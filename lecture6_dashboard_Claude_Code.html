Time-Differencing Schemes

Euler, Backward, Trapezoidal, Matsuno, Heun, Leapfrog, Adams-Bashforth — stability, accuracy, and the computational mode

Concept 01

Why Do We Need Time-Differencing Schemes?

Computers can only do arithmetic — so how do we advance the atmosphere forward in time?

In weather and climate modeling, we have equations that describe how the atmosphere changes over time:

General ODE Formdψ/dt = f(ψ, t)

Here, ψ (psi) could be wind speed, temperature, pressure — any atmospheric variable. The left side says "how fast is ψ changing right now?" and the right side says "that depends on the current values of everything."

The fundamental problem: Computers cannot do calculus. They cannot compute "instantaneous rates of change." Computers can only do arithmetic — addition, subtraction, multiplication, division. So we need to approximate calculus using arithmetic. That's what "time-differencing schemes" are: recipes for advancing a solution forward in time using only arithmetic operations.

Think of it this way: You know where you are right now (ψ at time n). You know how fast things are changing right now (f). You want to figure out where you'll be a short time later (ψ at time n+1). Different schemes give you different recipes for making that prediction, and each recipe has different strengths and weaknesses.

Concept 02

The Setup — True Solution vs. Numerical Solution

A critical distinction that underlies everything in this lecture.

We have the continuous PDE (the "true" equation):

1D Advection Equation∂ψ/∂t + c · ∂ψ/∂x = 0

This says "a wave moves to the right at speed c without changing shape." It has a true solution ψ(nΔt, jΔx) that we could compute if we had infinite precision.

Then we have our numerical solution φjn — what the computer actually calculates. The superscript n means "at time step n" and the subscript j means "at grid point j."

The whole game: How close can we make φjn to the true ψ(nΔt, jΔx)? And will our numerical solution behave well (not blow up) over many time steps?

To study time-differencing schemes in isolation (without the complication of spatial derivatives), the lecture simplifies to an ordinary differential equation:

dψ/dt = f(ψ, t)

This lets us focus purely on how we step forward in time.

Concept 03

Classification of Time-Differencing Schemes

Schemes organized by two properties: how many time levels, and how many stages per step.

How many time levels are used?

  • Two-level schemes: Only need information from time n to compute time n+1. They relate φn+1 to φn.
  • Three-level (multi-level) schemes: Use information from two previous times (n and n−1) to compute n+1.

How many stages within a single step?

  • Single-stage: One calculation per time step. You plug in, compute, done.
  • Multi-stage (predictor-corrector): First make a rough estimate (the "predictor"), then use it to make a better calculation (the "corrector"). Two calculations per time step.

Important practical note: At the very first time step, you only have one set of initial conditions (φ0). So you must start with a two-level scheme. Only after you have computed φ1 do you have two time levels available, allowing you to switch to a three-level scheme like Leapfrog.

Concept 04

Two-Level Single-Stage Schemes

Euler, Backward, and Trapezoidal — the three simplest time-stepping methods.

Euler (Forward) Scheme

Euler Schemeφn+1 = φn + Δt · fn
  • Explicit — φn+1 only on left side, direct computation
  • O(Δt) — first-order accurate
  • Always unstable for oscillatory problems (|A| > 1)

"Where I'll be ≈ where I am + (rate of change) × (time step)"

Backward (Implicit) Scheme

Backward Schemeφn+1 = φn + Δt · fn+1
  • Implicit — f evaluated at future time (must solve equations)
  • O(Δt) — first-order accurate
  • Always stable (unconditionally), but damps the solution

Evaluates tendency at the future time — requires equation solving.

Trapezoidal Scheme

Trapezoidal Schemeφn+1 = φn + (Δt/2)(fn + fn+1)
  • Implicit — averages present and future tendencies
  • O(Δt²) — second-order accurate!
  • Always neutral — neither amplifies nor damps (|A| = 1)

The "best of both worlds" — centered averaging of slopes at both endpoints.

Unified Formula for All Three

General Two-Level Single-Stageφn+1 = φn + Δt(α fn + β fn+1) with α + β = 1
SchemeαβExplicit/ImplicitAccuracy
Euler10ExplicitO(Δt)
Backward01ImplicitO(Δt)
Trapezoidal1/21/2ImplicitO(Δt²)

When α = 1 and β = 0, fn+1 disappears → Euler. When α = 0 and β = 1, fn disappears → Backward. When both are 1/2, you average → Trapezoidal.

Concept 05

The Oscillation Equation — The Universal Test Problem

Every scheme is tested against this equation because atmospheric dynamics are fundamentally oscillatory.

Why this specific equation?

In the real atmosphere, there are waves everywhere — sound waves, gravity waves, Rossby waves, inertial oscillations. All involve oscillatory behavior. The test problem is:

Oscillation Equationdψ/dt = iωψ

Where: ψ is a complex number, i = √−1 (imaginary unit), ω is the oscillation frequency.

Where does this come from physically?

Consider horizontal equations of motion with only the Coriolis force:

du/dt = fv     dv/dt = −fu

Define a complex variable U = u + iv. Then:

dU/dt = fv + i(−fu) = −if(u + iv) = −ifU

This is exactly the oscillation equation with ω = −f. The wind vector rotates in a circle due to the Coriolis effect — an inertial oscillation.

The true (exact) solution

Exact Solutionψ(t) = ψ(0) · eiωt = ψ(0) · [cos(ωt) + i·sin(ωt)]

The solution traces a circle in the complex plane:

  • The amplitude |ψ| stays constant (= |ψ(0)|) — never grows or shrinks
  • The phase advances by ωΔt per time step

Concept 06

Deriving the Amplification Factor — Step by Step

The mathematical heart of stability analysis, derived from first principles.

The setup

Apply the general scheme φn+1 = φn + Δt(αfn + βfn+1) to the oscillation equation f(ψ) = iωψ:

1

Substitute f = iωφ

φn+1 = φn + Δt(α · iωφn + β · iωφn+1)
2

Define μ = ωΔt and substitute

φn+1 = φn + iμ(αφn + βφn+1)
3

Collect terms — move all φn+1 to the left

φn+1(1 − iβμ) = φn(1 + iαμ)
4

Solve for the amplification factor A

A = φn+1/φn = (1 + iαμ) / (1 − iβμ)
5

Compute |A|² using conjugate

|A|² = (1 + α²μ²) / (1 + β²μ²)

Using (a+ib)(a−ib) = a² + b²

Plug in specific schemes

Euler (α=1, β=0)

|A|² = 1 + μ² > 1 always

Always unstable!

Backward (α=0, β=1)

|A|² = 1/(1 + μ²) < 1 always

Always stable (damping)!

Trapezoidal (α=β=½)

|A|² = (1+μ²/4)/(1+μ²/4) = 1

Always neutral!

Elegant insight: When more weight is on the present (α > β), the scheme amplifies. When more on the future (β > α), it damps. When equal, perfectly neutral.

Concept 07

Multi-Stage (Predictor-Corrector) Schemes

Getting the benefits of implicit schemes while keeping the simplicity of explicit ones.

The motivation

Among single-stage schemes: Euler is explicit but unstable, Backward/Trapezoidal are stable but implicit (expensive). Can we get stability while staying explicit?

Yes — through predictor-corrector methods:

  1. Predict: Use a cheap explicit method (Euler) to make a rough guess
  2. Correct: Use that guess to evaluate the tendency at the future time, then redo the calculation more carefully

7a. The Matsuno Scheme (Euler-Backward)

Step 1 — Predict (Euler)φ* = φn + Δt · fn
Step 2 — Correct (using predicted future)φn+1 = φn + Δt · f(φ*)

Accuracy: O(Δt) — first-order only. Stability: Much better than Euler!

Matsuno Amplification Factor

Applied to dψ/dt = iωψ with μ = ωΔt:

MatsunoA = 1 − μ² + iμ   →   |A|² = 1 − μ² + μ⁴

Stable when |A|² ≤ 1, which gives μ²(μ² − 1) ≤ 0, so μ ≤ 1 (i.e., ωΔt ≤ 1).

Maximum damping occurs at μ = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 where |A| ≈ 0.866. This selectively damps high-frequency noise — actually useful for cleaning up initial condition errors.

7b. The Heun Scheme (2nd-order Runge-Kutta)

Step 1 — Predict (Euler)φ* = φn + Δt · fn
Step 2 — Correct (average tendencies)φn+1 = φn + (Δt/2)(fn + f(φ*))

Accuracy: O(Δt²) — second-order! The averaging mimics Trapezoidal.

Heun Amplification Factor

HeunA = (1 − μ²/2) + iμ   →   |A|² = 1 + μ⁴/4

Always unstable! But the growth is proportional to μ⁴ (fourth power) — extremely weak. For μ = 0.1: Euler grows by 1% per step, Heun by only 0.0025%.

The dashed green line at |A|=1 is the perfect neutral reference. Notice Heun (purple) barely deviates from 1, while Euler (red) shoots upward rapidly.

Concept 08

Why Implicit Schemes Are Always Stable

The physical intuition behind the mathematical guarantee.

Both the Backward and Trapezoidal schemes (implicit) are stable regardless of Δt. Why?

Mathematical answer: In |A|² = (1 + α²μ²)/(1 + β²μ²), when β > 0 (implicit weight), the denominator grows with μ², counteracting the numerator's growth.

Physical intuition: Implicit schemes incorporate information about the future state into the computation. By "knowing" something about where the solution is going, they can self-correct. It's like driving while looking ahead through the windshield (implicit) versus driving only looking in the rearview mirror (explicit). Looking ahead gives you time to course-correct before problems develop.

The price: At every time step, you must solve a system of equations (because φn+1 appears on both sides). For 3D atmospheric models, this becomes very expensive.

Central trade-off: Stability versus computational cost — one of the central themes of the entire course.

Concept 09

Phase Error Analysis

Does the scheme propagate waves at the correct speed?

The setup

The amplification factor A = |A| · eiθ where θ is the phase change per step. The true solution changes phase by μ = ωΔt per step. The ratio θ/μ tells us:

ConditionBehavior
θ/μ > 1Numerical wave moves faster — accelerating
θ/μ = 1Correct speed — perfect
θ/μ < 1Numerical wave moves slower — decelerating

Computing θ

θ = arctan(Aim / Are)   →   θ/μ = arctan(Aim/Are) / μ

Phase errors for each scheme

  • Euler: θ/μ = arctan(μ)/μ < 1 always. Decelerating.
  • Backward: Same magnitude: arctan(μ)/μ. Also decelerating.
  • Matsuno: θ/μ = atan2(μ, 1−μ²)/μ. Accelerating for small μ — the only scheme that speeds up waves.

Practical message: Phase errors from time differencing are usually not the biggest concern. Spatial differencing typically introduces much larger phase errors. What really matters most is stability — that determines whether your simulation survives.

Concept 10

Three-Level Schemes — Why Use More Than Two Time Levels?

Using two previous time levels to achieve centered differencing — second-order accuracy, explicitly.

With two-level schemes, we face a dilemma: explicit schemes that are both stable and second-order accurate don't really exist for oscillatory problems. The good second-order scheme (Trapezoidal) is implicit — computationally expensive.

Clever idea: What if we use information from TWO previous time levels? After computing the first step, we have values at both time n and time n−1 in memory. Why not use both?

φn+1 = f(φn, φn−1)

The benefit: By spanning from n−1 to n+1 (total 2Δt), we achieve centered differencing in time → second-order accuracy, and we can do this explicitly.

The cost: Using three time levels introduces a fundamentally new problem — computational modes — that doesn't exist in two-level schemes.

Concept 11

The Leapfrog Scheme

The workhorse of atmospheric modeling — explicit, second-order, and neutral.

Leapfrog Schemeφn+1 = φn−1 + 2Δt · fn

"The value at the future time equals the value at the time before the present, plus twice the time step times the tendency at the present time."

The scheme "leaps" from n−1 over the present time n to land at n+1. The tendency fn is evaluated right at the center — this centering gives accuracy.

  • Second-order accurate: O(Δt²)
  • Explicit: no equations to solve
  • Probably the most widely used scheme in atmospheric models

But there's a catch — the computational mode (next section).

Concept 12

The Computational Mode — The Hidden Danger

Three-level schemes always produce two solution modes — one physical, one parasitic.

Why does a computational mode exist?

Leapfrog applied to dψ/dt = iωψ gives: φn+1 = φn−1 + 2iμφn

Writing φn+1 = Aφn and φn−1 = φn/A, we get:

Quadratic in AA² − 2iμA − 1 = 0

A quadratic has two roots. This is fundamental: a three-level scheme produces two amplification factors, not one.

Solving the quadratic

Using A = [2iμ ± 2√(1−μ²)] / 2:

A1 = iμ + √(1−μ²)  — Physical mode

As μ→0: A1 → 1 + iμ − μ²/2 + … ≈ eiμ ✓

|A1|² = μ² + (1−μ²) = 1

A2 = iμ − √(1−μ²)  — Computational mode

As μ→0: A2 → −1 (no physical counterpart!)

|A2|² = μ² + (1−μ²) = 1 (persists forever!)

What does the computational mode do?

Since A2 ≈ −1, the computational mode alternates sign every step:

φcompn ∝ (−1)n × (something)

This creates a zigzag pattern — completely unphysical. And since |A2| = 1, it never decays.

Stability requires μ ≤ 1. When μ > 1, √(1−μ²) becomes imaginary → |A| > 1 → blow-up.

Concept 13

Where Do Computational Modes Come From?

The mathematical root cause — and a guitar string analogy.

The mathematical root cause

For a two-level scheme: φn+1 = A · φn — a first-order recurrence with exactly one solution mode.

For a three-level scheme like Leapfrog: φn+1 = 2iμφn + φn−1 — a second-order recurrence with two independent solution modes.

General rule: An N-level scheme produces an (N−1)-degree polynomial in A, which has (N−1) roots. Only one root corresponds to the true physical solution. The remaining N−2 roots are all computational modes.

Guitar string analogy: If you pluck a string (the physical initial condition), it vibrates at the natural frequency (the physical mode). But the string also supports higher harmonics — overtones that ring simultaneously. In a two-level scheme, there's only room for one "harmonic." In a three-level scheme, there's room for two, and the second one (the computational mode) is like a parasitic overtone with no physical basis.

Concept 14

The Asselin-Robert Time Filter

Taming the computational mode — the standard tool used in atmospheric models.

Since the computational mode doesn't decay on its own (|A2| = 1), we need to actively suppress it. The filter replaces φn with a smoothed value:

Asselin-Robert Filterφ̃n = φn + (ν/2)(φn−1 − 2φn + φn+1)

where ν is a small filter coefficient, typically 0.01 to 0.1.

Why does this work?

The expression (φn−1 − 2φn + φn+1) is a second-order centered difference in time. For a smooth physical solution, this quantity is small (proportional to ∂²φ/∂t²).

But for the computational mode zigzag (+1, −1, +1, −1...), consecutive values differ dramatically:

φn−1 − 2φn + φn+1 = +1 − 2(−1) + 1 = +4

This large correction nudges φn toward the average of its neighbors, smoothing the zigzag. For the physical mode, the correction is tiny.

The cost: The filter reduces Leapfrog from second-order to effectively first-order accuracy. But with ν = 0.05, the degradation is small — an acceptable price for removing the computational mode.

Concept 15

The Adams-Bashforth Scheme

Another three-level scheme — extrapolation forward using two previous tendencies.

Adams-Bashforth Schemeφn+1 = φn + Δt(3⁄2 · fn − 1⁄2 · fn−1)

Notice the difference from Leapfrog:

  • Leapfrog steps from φn−1 to φn+1 using fn
  • Adams-Bashforth steps from φn to φn+1 using a weighted combination of fn and fn−1

The weighting (3/2 and −1/2) achieves second-order accuracy through extrapolation: more weight on the most recent tendency, minus a correction from the older one.

Properties

  • Second-order accurate: O(Δt²)
  • Explicit: only φn+1 is unknown
  • Also has a computational mode (three-level scheme)
  • Steps from φn (not φn−1) — more of an extrapolation than a "leap"

Choosing a Scheme for Atmospheric Models

No perfect scheme exists — the art is choosing the right combination for your problem.

How atmospheric models actually use these in practice

Step 1 (Startup): The first time step uses a two-level scheme like Matsuno or Runge-Kutta. You must start with a two-level scheme because you only have φ0. Matsuno is preferred because its damping cleans up IC noise.

Steps 2 onward (Main integration): Switch to Leapfrog — explicit (fast), second-order, neutral. Apply the Asselin-Robert filter to control the computational mode.

Special treatment for diffusion: Physical diffusion terms use the implicit Backward or Trapezoidal scheme, because the diffusion stability constraint (Δt ∝ Δx²) would force impractically small time steps explicitly.

Semi-implicit methods: Fast waves (sound, gravity) treated implicitly with Trapezoidal; slow terms (advection) treated explicitly with Leapfrog. This allows much larger time steps.

Deep Takeaways

Takeaway 1

Stability > Accuracy

An unstable scheme, no matter how accurate, will produce garbage. You cannot out-refine instability.

Takeaway 2

No Perfect Scheme

Every scheme involves trade-offs: explicit vs. implicit, stable vs. accurate, simple vs. mode-free.

Takeaway 3

Oscillation Eq. Is Universal

dψ/dt = iωψ reveals the essential character of any time-stepping method.

Takeaway 4

Three-Level = Comp. Modes

Leapfrog is the workhorse, but requires the Asselin filter to stay clean.

Takeaway 5

Phase Errors Are Secondary

Spatial differencing errors are typically larger than time-differencing phase errors.

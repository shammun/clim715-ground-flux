Basic Euler Equations & Waves

Lecture 4 — Sound waves, gravity waves, Rossby waves, and the practical problem they create for numerical modeling

Concept 01

Why Simplify? The Multiscale Problem

Before we write any equations, let's understand why this lecture exists.

The real atmosphere is incredibly complex. Energy flows through it at every scale — from tiny turbulent eddies (meters) to planetary waves (thousands of kilometers). There's density stratification (air gets thinner as you go up), wind shear (wind speed changes with height), terrain effects, moisture, rotation of the Earth... it's overwhelming.

So the first move of any scientist facing a system this complex is: strip it down to its bare essentials and see what behaviors emerge from the simplest possible version.

That's what this lecture does. We're going to take the full governing equations of the atmosphere and make a series of simplifying assumptions to get the simplest possible model that still captures the fundamental wave motions. Then we'll discover what kinds of waves this simplified atmosphere can support — and that discovery has enormous practical consequences for how we build numerical models.

Guitar string analogy: Think of it like this: if you want to understand how a guitar string vibrates, you don't start with a full 3D simulation of the wood, the air, the room acoustics, and the musician's fingers. You start with an idealized string under tension. Once you understand that, you add complexity back piece by piece.

The Multiscale Challenge

Concept 02

The Simplifying Assumptions

We build a toy atmosphere by making these simplifications:

2D only (x-z plane)

We ignore the y-direction entirely. Imagine looking at a vertical slice of the atmosphere — like cutting a cake and looking at the cross-section. This means we only consider horizontal motion (left-right, which we call u) and vertical motion (up-down, which we call w).

Completely dry

No moisture, no clouds, no rain. Water vapor complicates things enormously because when it condenses it releases heat, which changes everything. We'll add moisture back later in the course.

Adiabatic

No heating or cooling from radiation, from the ground, or from any external source. The only temperature changes come from air parcels being compressed or expanded as they move vertically.

No rotation (no Coriolis)

We ignore that the Earth is spinning. The Coriolis effect is what makes hurricanes spin and creates the jet stream, but for understanding basic wave motions we don't need it yet.

No friction

Air parcels slide freely without any drag or viscosity.

No diffusion

No turbulent mixing.

The remarkable result: Even with all these simplifications, the resulting equations still produce remarkably rich behavior — they support three different kinds of waves, which is the main discovery of this lecture.

Concept 03

The Four Euler Equations

Now, what equations describe this simplified atmosphere? We need equations that express three fundamental physical laws plus one relationship.

Equation 1 — Horizontal Momentum (Newton's 2nd Law in x-direction)

Horizontal Momentumdu/dt + (1/ρ) ∂p/∂x = 0

Let me unpack every single symbol and what this equation says physically.

  • u is the horizontal wind speed (m/s).
  • du/dt is how that wind speed changes over time — the acceleration of the air parcel.
  • ρ (rho) is air density (kg/m³).
  • p is pressure (Pa).
  • ∂p/∂x is how pressure changes as you move horizontally.

The term (1/ρ)(∂p/∂x) is the pressure gradient force per unit mass in the horizontal direction. If pressure is higher on the left and lower on the right, then ∂p/∂x is negative (pressure decreases as x increases), and the force pushes the air to the right (toward lower pressure).

So this equation says: the horizontal acceleration of an air parcel equals the horizontal pressure gradient force (with a minus sign because high-to-low pressure pushes in the negative gradient direction).

It's Newton's F = ma, but written as a = F/m, where the only force is the pressure gradient.

Equation 2 — Vertical Momentum (Newton's 2nd Law in z-direction)

Vertical Momentumdw/dt + (1/ρ) ∂p/∂z + g = 0

This is the same idea but vertically. w is vertical wind speed. There are now two forces: the vertical pressure gradient (1/ρ)(∂p/∂z) pushing upward (because pressure decreases with height), and gravity g pulling downward.

This equation says: the vertical acceleration of an air parcel equals the net of the upward pressure gradient force minus gravity.

In the normal atmosphere, these two nearly cancel (that's hydrostatic balance from Lecture 3), and vertical motions are very small compared to horizontal ones.

Equation 3 — Thermodynamic Equation (Conservation of Energy)

Thermodynamicdθ/dt = 0

This is beautifully simple. θ is potential temperature — the temperature an air parcel would have if you brought it adiabatically to a reference pressure level (1000 mb). Since we assumed no heating or cooling (adiabatic), potential temperature is conserved — it doesn't change as the parcel moves around. dθ/dt = 0 means "θ stays constant following the parcel."

Equation 4 — Continuity Equation (Conservation of Mass)

Continuitydρ/dt + ρ ∇·V = 0

This says mass is neither created nor destroyed. If air converges into a region (∇·V < 0, meaning more air flowing in than out), then density must increase (dρ/dt > 0). If air diverges (spreads out), density decreases.

Plus the Equation of State (Ideal Gas Law)

Equation of Statep = ρ Rd Tv

This isn't a "new" equation in the sense of expressing a physical law — it's a thermodynamic relationship connecting pressure, density, and temperature. It's what closes the system (gives us enough equations for the number of unknowns).

The Material Derivative d/dt

One crucial thing to clarify. The notation d/dt here is the material derivative (also called the total or Lagrangian derivative):

Material (Lagrangian) Derivatived/dt = ∂/∂t + u ∂/∂x + w ∂/∂z

This measures the rate of change following the moving air parcel, not at a fixed point. In our 2D simplification (no y-direction), it only has x and z terms.

The Four Euler Equations — Overview

Concept 04

Two Kinds of Phenomena — Advection vs. Waves

Here's a key insight from the lecture that you need to internalize before we go further.

These Euler equations describe two fundamentally different kinds of motion:

Advection is when stuff gets carried along by the wind. Imagine dropping a leaf into a river — it moves downstream with the current. In the atmosphere, temperature, moisture, pollutants all get advected (carried) by the wind. Advection always moves things downstream, in the direction the wind is blowing.

Waves are oscillatory disturbances that can propagate independently of the mean wind. Unlike advection, waves can travel upstream (against the wind), downstream (with the wind), or even stay stationary. A familiar example: you drop a stone into a pond, and ripples spread outward in all directions regardless of whether there's a current.

The crucial question this lecture answers is: What kinds of waves do these simplified Euler equations support?

The answer turns out to be three types: sound waves, gravity waves, and (if we add back Earth's rotation) Rossby waves. Each travels at a dramatically different speed, and this has enormous consequences for numerical modeling.

Concept 05

The Perturbation Method — The Key Mathematical Trick

Before we can find the waves, we need a powerful mathematical tool: the perturbation method. This is perhaps the single most important technique in this lecture, and it's used throughout atmospheric science.

The Core Idea

The core idea: Any atmospheric variable can be split into a mean part (the background state) and a perturbation (a small deviation from that background):

The Perturbation Decompositiona = ā + a′

For example, the wind speed at any point is: u = ū + u′, where ū might be a steady 10 m/s background wind, and u′ is a small fluctuation (maybe ±0.5 m/s) on top of that.

Why is this useful? Because the perturbations are small compared to the mean, we can make a massive simplification: we can throw away any terms that multiply two perturbations together (like u′·p′ or u′²). Since each perturbation is small, the product of two small things is really small — negligibly so.

This process is called linearization, and it transforms the original nonlinear equations (which are basically impossible to solve analytically) into much simpler linear equations (which we can solve).

Key Properties of Perturbations

Property 1: The average of a perturbation is zero:

avg(a′) = 0

This makes sense — the perturbation is the deviation from the mean, so deviations above the mean exactly cancel deviations below the mean when averaged.

Property 2: Products of perturbations are NOT zero:

avg(a′ b′) ≠ 0

This is subtle but critical. Even though avg(a′) = 0 and avg(b′) = 0, their product can have a non-zero average. Think of it this way: if temperature fluctuations and vertical wind fluctuations are correlated (warm air tends to rise), then avg(w′θ′) is positive even though both avg(w′) = 0 and avg(θ′) = 0.

These products (avg(u′w′), avg(w′θ′), etc.) represent turbulent fluxes — the transport of momentum, heat, and moisture by turbulence. They're enormously important in PBL physics (Lecture 9), but for now, we're going to throw them away to get our linearized wave equations.

Property 3: When we linearize (drop products of perturbations), the equations become linear, which means the principle of superposition applies — we can study individual waves one at a time and add them up later.

Concept 06

The Spectral Gap — Why Linearization Works

You might wonder: is it really justified to throw away those perturbation products? The answer comes from an observational fact called the spectral gap.

If you measure wind speed over time at a single point and plot the energy at each frequency (a power spectrum), you'll see three peaks: one at large scales (synoptic weather systems, ~days), one at the diurnal cycle (~24 hours), and one at microscale turbulence (~seconds to minutes). Between the synoptic and turbulent peaks, there's a gap — a region of frequencies where very little energy exists.

This gap lets us cleanly separate the atmosphere into "resolved" motions (larger than the gap, which we compute explicitly) and "unresolved" motions (smaller than the gap, which we parameterize). Most NWP models use grid spacings that fall right in this gap.

For our wave analysis, this gap justifies treating the mean state as slowly varying and the perturbations as small, rapid fluctuations on top of it.

Concept 07

Setting Up the Sound Wave Problem

What Do We Keep and What Do We Drop?

Sound waves are horizontal compressions and expansions of air. Picture clapping your hands — you squeeze the air between your palms, creating a region of slightly higher pressure and density. That compressed region pushes on the neighboring air, which compresses it, and so on — the disturbance propagates outward as a pressure pulse.

The key physical features of sound waves are:

  • They involve horizontal motion (air molecules vibrate left and right)
  • They involve pressure and density fluctuations (compressions and rarefactions)
  • They do NOT involve vertical motion — a horizontal sound wave doesn't make air move up or down
  • They are longitudinal waves: the air molecules vibrate in the same direction as the wave propagates

Because sound waves don't involve vertical motion, we can drop the vertical momentum equation (the dw/dt equation) entirely. We also drop the vertical component from our system — everything happens in the horizontal x direction only.

This leaves us with three equations:

Horizontal Momentumdu/dt + (1/ρ) ∂p/∂x = 0
Thermodynamic — θ is conserveddθ/dt = 0
Continuity — mass conservationdρ/dt + ρ ∂u/∂x = 0

And three unknowns: u (wind), p (pressure), and ρ (density), with θ related to p and ρ through the definition of potential temperature.

Our goal is to reduce this to two equations in two unknowns, then combine them into a single wave equation, and finally extract the wave speed.

Concept 08

Eliminating θ — The Algebraic Trick

We have three equations but we want two. The thermodynamic equation dθ/dt = 0 looks simple, but θ is defined in terms of p and T (and T relates to p and ρ via the ideal gas law). So we can use dθ/dt = 0 to create a relationship between dp/dt and dρ/dt, effectively eliminating θ from the system.

Here's the step-by-step derivation:

Step 1 — Definition of potential temperature
θ = T (p₀/p)Rd/cpd
Step 2 — Replace T using ideal gas law

Use p = ρRdTv to replace T. Solving for T gives T = p/(ρRd) (ignoring the small moisture correction since our atmosphere is dry):

θ = [p / (ρ Rd)] · (p₀/p)Rd/cpd
Step 3 — Simplify the powers of p

Note that p appears twice — once as p in the numerator and once as p−Rd/cpd from the bracket. Combining:

θ = (p₀Rd/cpd / ρRd) · p1 − Rd/cpd

Now, 1 − Rd/cpd = (cpd − Rd)/cpd = cvd/cpd (because for an ideal gas, cpd − Rd = cvd). So:

θ = (p₀Rd/cpd / ρRd) · pcvd/cpd
Step 4 — Take the natural log of both sides
ln θ = const + (cvd/cpd) ln p − ln ρ

(The "const" absorbs the p₀ and Rd terms since they're constants.)

Step 5 — Differentiate following the parcel (apply d/dt)
d(ln θ)/dt = (cvd/cpd) d(ln p)/dt − d(ln ρ)/dt
Step 6 — Apply dθ/dt = 0 (left side vanishes)

Since dθ/dt = 0 (our thermodynamic equation), and since d(ln θ)/dt = (1/θ)(dθ/dt) = 0, the left side is zero:

0 = (cvd/cpd) d(ln p)/dt − d(ln ρ)/dt

This is a beautiful result! It says that for adiabatic motion, changes in pressure and density are locked together in a specific ratio. This relationship is what makes sound waves possible — pressure and density oscillate together.

Step 7 — Eliminate dρ/dt using continuity

Recall that d(ln ρ)/dt = (1/ρ)(dρ/dt), and the continuity equation says dρ/dt = −ρ(∂u/∂x). So:

d(ln ρ)/dt = (1/ρ)(dρ/dt) = −∂u/∂x

Substituting into the boxed equation above:

0 = (cvd/cpd) d(ln p)/dt + ∂u/∂x
Step 8 — Rearrange, define γ = cpd/cvd ≈ 1.4
(1/γ) d(ln p)/dt + ∂u/∂x = 0

Since d(ln p)/dt = (1/p)(dp/dt), this becomes:

(1/γp) dp/dt + ∂u/∂x = 0

Or equivalently:

dp/dt + pγ ∂u/∂x = 0

Expanding the material derivative dp/dt = ∂p/∂t + u·∂p/∂x:

∂p/∂t + u ∂p/∂x + pγ ∂u/∂x = 0

Result: Two Nonlinear Equations

We've now reduced four equations to two:

Momentum∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x + (1/ρ) ∂p/∂x = 0
Pressure∂p/∂t + u ∂p/∂x + pγ ∂u/∂x = 0

Two equations, two unknowns (u and p). But they're still nonlinear (notice the u·∂u/∂x term — the unknown u multiplied by its own derivative). We need to linearize them using the perturbation method.

Concept 09

Linearizing — Applying the Perturbation Method to Sound Waves

Now we split each variable into mean + perturbation, substitute into the equations, and drop products of perturbations.

Now we split each variable into mean + perturbation:

u = ū + u′,   p = p̄ + p′,   ρ = ρ̄ + ρ′

where the bars are constants (steady, uniform background state) and the primes are small fluctuations.

Since the mean values are constants, their spatial and time derivatives are zero: ∂ū/∂t = 0, ∂ū/∂x = 0, ∂p̄/∂x = 0, etc.

Substitution into the Horizontal Momentum Equation

∂(ū+u′)/∂t + (ū+u′)∂(ū+u′)/∂x + [1/(ρ̄+ρ′)] ∂(p̄+p′)/∂x = 0

Expanding term by term:

First term

∂ū/∂t + ∂u′/∂t = 0 + ∂u′/∂t

Second term

(ū + u′)(∂ū/∂x + ∂u′/∂x) = (ū + u′)(0 + ∂u′/∂x) = ū·∂u′/∂x + u′·∂u′/∂x

The term u′·∂u′/∂x is a product of two perturbations — this is negligibly small, so we DROP it.

Third term — the 1/(1+x) ≈ 1−x trick

We need a trick for 1/(ρ̄ + ρ′). Using the Taylor series approximation for small ρ′/ρ̄:

1/(ρ̄+ρ′) = (1/ρ̄) · 1/(1 + ρ′/ρ̄) ≈ (1/ρ̄)(1 − ρ′/ρ̄)

This is the same idea as 1/(1+x) ≈ 1−x for small x. So:

[1/(ρ̄+ρ′)] ∂(p̄+p′)/∂x ≈ (1/ρ̄)(1 − ρ′/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂x

The (ρ′/ρ̄)·∂p′/∂x term is again a product of two perturbations — DROP it.

Linearized Momentum Equation

Linearized Momentum∂u′/∂t + ū ∂u′/∂x + (1/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂x = 0

Linearized Pressure Equation

Following identical logic (expanding, dropping products of perturbations, noting ∂p̄/∂x = 0):

Linearized Pressure∂p′/∂t + ū ∂p′/∂x + p̄γ ∂u′/∂x = 0

These are the linearized sound wave equations. Notice how clean they are — everything is now in terms of the perturbation quantities u′ and p′, with constant coefficients ū, ρ̄, p̄, and γ. These are linear equations, which means we can solve them exactly.

Concept 10

Deriving the Wave Equation for Sound

Now comes the exciting part — combining those two linearized equations into a single wave equation, then solving it.

Forming a Single Equation for p′

We have:

Eq. A — Momentum∂u′/∂t + ū ∂u′/∂x + (1/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂x = 0
Eq. B — Pressure∂p′/∂t + ū ∂p′/∂x + p̄γ ∂u′/∂x = 0

The strategy is to eliminate u′ and get one equation purely in terms of p′.

The result can be written compactly as:

Wave Equation (expanded operator form)(∂/∂t + ū ∂/∂x)² p′ − (γp̄/ρ̄) ∂²p′/∂x² = 0

The operator (∂/∂t + ū·∂/∂x) appears twice (squared). This operator is actually the material derivative following the mean flow — it measures the rate of change as seen by an observer moving with the background wind ū. Let's call it D/Dt for short:

Classical Wave EquationD²p′/Dt² = cs² ∂²p′/∂x²

where cs² = γp̄/ρ̄ is the square of the sound speed. The wave equation says: the acceleration of the pressure perturbation (in the frame moving with the mean wind) is proportional to the curvature of the pressure perturbation in space.

Vibrating string analogy: If you've ever seen the wave equation for a vibrating string — ∂²y/∂t² = c²·∂²y/∂x² — this is the exact same structure. It guarantees wave-like solutions.

Concept 11

Solving the Wave Equation — The Wave Solution

We assume a wave-like solution, compute derivatives, substitute, and extract the phase speed.

Assumed Solution

We assume the solution has a wave-like form:

Wave Ansatzp′ = A eik(x − ct)

Every piece of this expression:

  • A is the amplitude — how strong the pressure fluctuation is
  • ei(…) is Euler's formula: eiφ = cos φ + i sin φ. So this represents an oscillating wave. The imaginary number i is just a mathematical convenience — the physical pressure is the real part.
  • k is the wavenumber: k = 2π/Lx, where Lx is the wavelength. A larger k means a shorter wavelength (more waves packed into the same distance).
  • c is the phase speed — how fast the wave crests move. This is what we're trying to find!
  • (x − ct) means the wave pattern moves to the right at speed c. At time t=0, the pattern looks like cos(kx). At time t=1, it looks like cos(k(x−c)) — the same pattern shifted rightward by distance c.

Computing the Required Derivatives

Taking derivatives of p′ = Aeik(x−ct):

Time derivative

Inside the exponent, the t part is −ikct, so:

∂p′/∂t = A · (ik)(−c) · eik(x−ct) = −ikc · p′
Space derivative

The x part of the exponent is ikx, so:

∂p′/∂x = ik · p′
Second space derivative
∂²p′/∂x² = (ik)² · p′ = −k² · p′

Substituting into the Wave Equation

The first bracket (material derivative applied to p′):

∂p′/∂t + ū ∂p′/∂x = −ikc · p′ + ū · ik · p′ = ik(ū − c) · p′

Applying the operator twice gives:

[ik(ū − c)]² · p′ = −k²(ū − c)² · p′

The second term:

−(γp̄/ρ̄) · (−k²) · p′ = (γp̄/ρ̄) k² · p′

Setting the whole thing to zero and dividing by p′ (which isn't zero) and by k²:

−(ū − c)² + γp̄/ρ̄ = 0   ⇒   (ū − c)² = γp̄/ρ̄
Concept 12

The Phase Speed of Sound — The Punchline

The first major result: sound waves travel at ~350 m/s in both directions, riding on the mean wind.

Taking the square root:

ū − c = ±√(γp̄/ρ̄)

Solving for c:

Phase Speed of Soundc = ū ± √(γp̄/ρ̄)

Using the ideal gas law p̄ = ρ̄RdT̄ to simplify γp̄/ρ̄ = γRdT̄:

Phase Speed of Sound (final form)c = ū ± √(γ Rd T̄)

This is the phase speed of sound waves in the atmosphere!

Physical Interpretation

The ū part: The sound wave is carried along by the background wind. If the wind is blowing at 10 m/s, the wave rides on top of that.

The ± sign: There are two sound waves — one propagating to the right (+) and one to the left (−). Sound radiates in both directions from its source.

The √(γRdT̄) part: This is the intrinsic speed of sound — the speed relative to the air itself. It depends only on temperature (and the gas constants). For a typical tropospheric temperature of T̄ = 300 K:

√(γ Rd T̄) = √(1.4 × 287 × 300) = √(120,540) ≈ 347 m/s

So sound travels at roughly 350 m/s (~1,260 km/hr, or about Mach 1).

Concept 13

Why Sound Waves Matter for Modeling — And Why They're a Problem

Here's the punchline that connects this physics to numerical modeling.

Sound waves travel at ~350 m/s. But the weather phenomena we actually care about — cyclones, fronts, convective systems — move at speeds of 10–30 m/s. Sound waves are roughly 10–30 times faster than the interesting meteorology.

Why is this a problem? Because of the CFL condition from Lecture 5. The CFL condition says:

CFL Stability ConstraintΔt ≤ Δx / cmax

The maximum time step you can use is limited by the fastest wave in your system. If your fastest wave is sound at 350 m/s (or even faster at higher temperatures), and your grid spacing is, say, 10 km = 10,000 m, then:

Δt ≤ 10,000 / 350 ≈ 29 seconds

You're forced to take tiny time steps just to keep sound waves stable, even though you don't care about sound waves at all! They carry no useful weather information — they just prevent vacuum holes from forming in the atmosphere.

This is why the lecture later discusses methods to eliminate or slow down sound waves (anelastic equation, time-splitting, implicit methods). It's one of the most important practical issues in atmospheric model design.

Concept 14

Sound Waves Are Longitudinal

One final physical property to internalize: sound waves are longitudinal waves.

This means the air molecules vibrate parallel to the direction the wave travels.

Slinky analogy: Picture a long slinky toy stretched out on a table. If you push one end, a compression pulse travels along the slinky — the coils bunch up, then spread apart, then bunch up again. The coils move left-right (same direction as the pulse travels), not up-down. That's a longitudinal wave.

In the atmosphere: a sound wave moving to the right causes air molecules to oscillate left-right. Pressure and density fluctuate together — where molecules are bunched (compressed), pressure is high; where they're spread apart (rarefied), pressure is low.

This is fundamentally different from gravity waves, which are transverse waves where the motion is perpendicular to the propagation direction.

Concept 15

What Are Gravity Waves? The Physical Picture

Gravity waves are the second type of wave hidden inside the Euler equations. This is physically very different from sound waves.

Imagine the atmosphere is stably stratified — meaning warmer (lighter) air sits on top of cooler (denser) air. This is the normal state of most of the atmosphere above the boundary layer. The potential temperature θ increases with height: dθ̄/dz > 0.

Now imagine you take an air parcel from some level and push it upward by a small distance. What happens?

  1. The parcel rises into a region of lower pressure (pressure always decreases with height).
  2. Lower pressure means the parcel expands.
  3. Expansion without heating (adiabatic) means the parcel cools — its temperature drops at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (about 9.8°C per km).
  4. But the environment at that higher level is warmer than the parcel has become (because the atmosphere is stably stratified — the environment cools more slowly with height than the adiabatic rate).
  5. So the displaced parcel is now cooler and denser than its surroundings.
  6. Being denser, gravity pulls it back down — it's a restoring force!

The parcel overshoots its original level, now finding itself in warmer surroundings again, so it's pushed back up. The result: the parcel oscillates up and down like a mass on a spring. The "spring constant" is the stability of the atmosphere — how strongly θ increases with height.

These vertical oscillations of air parcels are gravity waves. But here's the subtle part: while each parcel oscillates vertically, the wave pattern propagates horizontally. Think of a stadium wave — each person stands up and sits down (vertical motion), but the wave pattern sweeps sideways around the arena. Gravity waves are transverse waves: the particle motion (vertical) is perpendicular to the wave propagation (horizontal).

Concept 16

Setting Up the Gravity Wave Derivation

The setup is different from sound waves in several important ways.

For sound waves we worked in 1D (x only) and the key physics was compressibility — air getting squeezed and un-squeezed horizontally.

For gravity waves we need 2D (x and z) because the physics involves vertical displacement in a gravitationally stratified atmosphere. The key physics is buoyancy — the restoring force when a parcel is displaced vertically in a stable environment.

We also make one additional simplification that we didn't make for sound waves: we assume the atmosphere is incompressible. This is a deliberate choice — by removing compressibility, we filter out sound waves entirely and isolate gravity waves. The incompressible continuity equation is:

Incompressible Continuity∂u′/∂x + ∂w′/∂z = 0

instead of the full dρ/dt + ρ∇·V = 0. This simply says: if air converges horizontally (∂u′/∂x < 0), it must diverge vertically (∂w′/∂z > 0) to conserve volume. No density changes allowed.

Perturbation Setup

We split every variable into a mean (function of height only) and a perturbation:

u = ū(z) + u′(x,z,t)   w = 0 + w′(x,z,t)   θ = θ̄(z) + θ′(x,z,t)
p = p̄(z) + p′(x,z,t)   ρ = ρ̄(z) + ρ′(x,z,t)

Notice that the mean vertical wind w̄ is set to zero — the background state has no vertical motion. Also notice the means are functions of height z, not constants like they were for sound waves.

The Buoyancy Relation

From the perturbation analysis of the vertical momentum equation (using the Boussinesq approximation from Lecture 3):

Density-Temperature Linkρ′/ρ̄ ≈ −θ′/θ̄

This says: if a parcel is warmer than its surroundings (θ′ > 0), it is less dense (ρ′ < 0), and vice versa.

Concept 17

The Four Linearized Gravity Wave Equations

After applying the perturbation method and linearizing (dropping products of perturbations), and also assuming ū = 0 for simplicity (no background wind), we get four clean equations.

Equation 1 — Horizontal momentum

Horizontal Momentum∂u′/∂t = −(1/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂x

Horizontal acceleration is driven by the horizontal pressure perturbation gradient.

Equation 2 — Vertical momentum (the most important equation)

Vertical Momentum — with Buoyancy!∂w′/∂t = −(1/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂z + g θ′/θ̄

This is the most important equation. There are two forces on a vertically displaced parcel:

  • The vertical pressure perturbation gradient: −(1/ρ̄) ∂p′/∂z
  • The buoyancy force: g·θ′/θ̄. If the parcel is warm (θ′ > 0), buoyancy pushes it up. If cool (θ′ < 0), buoyancy pushes it down.

This buoyancy term is what makes gravity waves possible. Sound waves had no buoyancy — they were purely pressure-driven.

Equation 3 — Incompressible continuity

Incompressible Continuity∂u′/∂x + ∂w′/∂z = 0

Volume is conserved. Horizontal convergence equals vertical divergence.

Equation 4 — Thermodynamic

Thermodynamic∂θ′/∂t + w′ dθ̄/dz = 0

This says: the perturbation potential temperature at a fixed point changes because the vertical wind w′ advects the background θ profile. If the background θ increases with height (dθ̄/dz > 0, stable stratification) and there's upward motion (w′ > 0), then cooler air from below is being brought to this level, so θ′ decreases (∂θ′/∂t < 0).

In other words: vertical motion in a stratified atmosphere automatically creates temperature perturbations, and those temperature perturbations create buoyancy forces, which drive further vertical motion. This feedback loop is the gravity wave mechanism.

The feedback loop: Vertical motion → creates θ′ → creates buoyancy force → drives more vertical motion → and so on. This self-sustaining oscillation IS the gravity wave.

Concept 18

Deriving the Gravity Wave Equation — The Algebra

Now we combine these four equations into a single wave equation for w′. The strategy is to systematically eliminate u′, p′, and θ′, leaving only w′.

Step 1 — Take partial derivatives needed for elimination

Take ∂/∂x of Equation 2 (vertical momentum):

(∂/∂t)(∂w′/∂x) = −(1/ρ̄) ∂²p′/∂x∂z + (g/θ̄) ∂θ′/∂x

Take ∂/∂z of Equation 1 (horizontal momentum):

(∂/∂t)(∂u′/∂z) = −(1/ρ̄) ∂²p′/∂z∂x
Step 2 — Subtract to cancel pressure

The pressure terms cancel! (Because ∂²p′/∂x∂z = ∂²p′/∂z∂x — mixed partials are equal for smooth functions.)

(∂/∂t)(∂w′/∂x − ∂u′/∂z) = (g/θ̄) ∂θ′/∂x
Step 3 — Differentiate with respect to t

Use Equation 4 to replace ∂θ′/∂t = −w′(dθ̄/dz):

(∂²/∂t²)(∂w′/∂x − ∂u′/∂z) = −(g/θ̄)(dθ̄/dz) ∂w′/∂x
Step 4 — Use continuity to express everything in terms of w′

Since ∂u′/∂x = −∂w′/∂z, we can take appropriate derivatives and substitute. After careful manipulation, we arrive at:

The Gravity Wave Equation(∂²/∂t²)(∂²w′/∂x² + ∂²w′/∂z²) + N² ∂²w′/∂x² = 0

where N is the Brunt-Väisälä frequency defined in the next section.

Concept 19

The Brunt-Väisälä Frequency N

One of the most fundamental parameters in atmospheric science — the natural oscillation frequency of a displaced air parcel.

Brunt-Väisälä FrequencyN ≡ √[(g/θ̄)(dθ̄/dz)] = √[g · d(ln θ̄)/dz]

This is the Brunt-Väisälä buoyancy frequency, and it is one of the most fundamental parameters in atmospheric science.

N is the natural frequency of oscillation of a vertically displaced air parcel in a stably stratified atmosphere. It's like the frequency of a pendulum or a mass on a spring — it tells you how many times per second the parcel bobs up and down.

What Determines N

  • g (gravity, 9.8 m/s²): Stronger gravity means a stronger restoring force, so faster oscillations, so larger N.
  • dθ̄/dz (rate of increase of potential temperature with height): This is the stability of the atmosphere. If θ increases rapidly with height (strongly stable), the displaced parcel feels a strong restoring force and oscillates quickly — large N. If θ barely increases (weakly stable), oscillations are slow — small N. If dθ̄/dz < 0 (unstable), N² is negative, meaning no oscillation — the parcel just keeps accelerating (convection!).
  • θ̄ (background potential temperature, ~300 K): This just normalizes things.

Typical Values

In the troposphere, N ≈ 0.01 s−1. This means the oscillation period is:

P = 2π/N = 2π/0.01 ≈ 628 seconds ≈ 10 minutes

Physical Interpretation of the Three Regimes

  • N² > 0 (stable): θ increases with height. Displaced parcels oscillate → gravity waves exist.
  • N² = 0 (neutral): θ is constant with height. No restoring force. Displaced parcels stay where they are.
  • N² < 0 (unstable): θ decreases with height. Displaced parcels accelerate away → convection, thunderstorms. N is imaginary, no oscillation.

Concept 20

Solving the Gravity Wave Equation — Dispersion Relation

Assume a 2D wave solution, substitute, and extract the relationship between frequency and wavenumber.

Assumed 2D Wave Solution

Now we solve the gravity wave equation. We assume a wave-like solution with 2D spatial structure:

Wave Ansatzw′ = A ei(kx + mz − ωt)

Where:

  • k = 2π/Lx is the horizontal wavenumber
  • m = 2π/Lz is the vertical wavenumber (gravity waves have structure in both dimensions)
  • ω = 2π/P is the frequency
  • A is the amplitude

Computing the Required Derivatives

∂²w′/∂x² = (ik)² w′ = −k² w′
∂²w′/∂z² = (im)² w′ = −m² w′

Since ∂w′/∂t = −iω · w′, we get ∂²w′/∂t² = −ω² w′. Therefore:

(∂²/∂t²)[−(k²+m²)w′] = −(k²+m²)(−ω²)w′ = ω²(k²+m²)w′

And:

N² ∂²w′/∂x² = N²(−k²)w′ = −N²k²w′

Substituting into the Gravity Wave Equation

ω²(k² + m²)w′ − N²k²w′ = 0

Divide by w′:

ω²(k² + m²) = N²k²

Solve for ω:

Gravity Wave Dispersion Relationω = ± Nk / √(k² + m²)

This is the gravity wave dispersion relation!

Concept 21

Gravity Wave Phase Speed — The Physical Result

Gravity waves travel at ~15 m/s — about 20 times slower than sound — and they are dispersive.

The phase speed in the horizontal direction:

cx = ω/k = ± N / √(k² + m²)

Adding back the mean wind ū:

Gravity Wave Phase Speedcx = ū ± N / √(k² + m²)

Typical Value

For standard tropospheric conditions (N = 0.01 s−1, horizontal wavelength Lx = 10 km, m = 0):

cx = N/k = 0.01 / (6.28 × 10−4) ≈ 16 m/s

So gravity waves travel at roughly 15 m/s — about 20 times slower than sound waves!

Key Properties

Property 1: Gravity waves are dispersive. The phase speed depends on wavenumber. Different wavelengths travel at different speeds, unlike non-dispersive sound waves.

Property 2: The frequency is bounded by N. Since k/√(k²+m²) ≤ 1, we have ω ≤ N. The Brunt-Väisälä frequency is the maximum possible frequency for gravity waves.

Property 3: Longer waves are faster. Smaller k (longer wavelength) gives a larger phase speed.

Property 4: Vertical structure slows the wave. Adding vertical wavenumber m increases √(k²+m²), which decreases the phase speed.

Sound vs. Gravity Wave Speed Comparison

Concept 22

Sound and Gravity Waves Happen Simultaneously

When an air parcel is displaced vertically, both kinds of waves are triggered simultaneously.

The displaced parcel needs to compress the neighboring air to make room for itself. That compression signal travels outward as a sound wave at ~350 m/s — almost instantaneously relative to everything else.

At the same time, if the atmosphere is stable, the displaced parcel begins to oscillate vertically under buoyancy, creating a gravity wave that radiates horizontally at ~15 m/s — roughly at the speed of the flow itself.

So the environment response to a vertical displacement has two components:

  1. Fast horizontal adjustment via sound waves (pressure equilibrates almost instantly)
  2. Slow vertical oscillation via gravity waves (buoyancy restores the parcel over minutes)
Concept 23

Rossby Waves — The Third Wave Type

Rossby waves are the third and slowest type of wave supported by the atmospheric equations. To get them, we need to add back the rotation of the Earth — specifically, the variation of the Coriolis parameter with latitude, called beta (β).

The Coriolis Parameter

The Earth is spinning. The Coriolis parameter describes the strength of the apparent deflection of moving objects:

Coriolis Parameterf = 2Ω sin φ

where Ω is Earth's rotation rate (7.29 × 10−5 rad/s) and φ is latitude. At the equator, f = 0; at the poles, f is maximum.

The β-Effect

The rate at which f changes with latitude:

Beta Parameterβ = df/dy = (2Ω cos φ) / Rearth

At mid-latitudes (45°N), β ≈ 1.6 × 10−11 m−1s−1.

The Physical Mechanism

Imagine a parcel of air sitting at some latitude. It has absolute vorticity f + ζ (planetary + relative), which is conserved:

Vorticity Conservationf + ζ = constant following the parcel
  • Parcel pushed northward → f increases → ζ must decrease (anticyclonic spin) → curves back south
  • Parcel pushed southward → f decreases → ζ must increase (cyclonic spin) → curves back north

The result: meridional oscillations — Rossby waves. These are the dominant wave pattern of the large-scale atmosphere, the familiar troughs and ridges on a 500 mb weather map, with wavelengths of 4,000 to 6,000 km.

Concept 24

Rossby Wave Phase Speed

The Rossby wave phase speed (presented without full derivation):

Rossby Wave Phase Speedc = ū − β/k²

Physical Interpretation

ū: The mean zonal (west-to-east) wind speed carries the wave along.

−β/k²: The intrinsic Rossby wave phase speed. The minus sign means Rossby waves propagate westward relative to the mean flow — fundamentally different from sound and gravity waves which go both directions.

k² in the denominator: Longer wavelengths (smaller k) have stronger westward intrinsic speed.

The Stationary Wave Condition

A Rossby wave is stationary (c = 0) when:

ū = β/k²

For 3 troughs/ridges around the hemisphere (Lx ≈ 6,000 km), this gives ū ≈ 15 m/s — remarkably close to typical upper-tropospheric jet stream speeds.

Direction of Movement

  • ū < β/k²: Waves drift westward (retrograde)
  • ū = β/k²: Waves are stationary
  • ū > β/k²: Waves drift eastward (progressive)

Concept 26

The CFL Problem — Why Sound Waves Must Be Dealt With

The CFL condition says the maximum time step is limited by the fastest wave.

CFL ConditionΔt ≤ Δx / cmax

The fastest wave (cmax) is sound at ~350 m/s. For a grid spacing of Δx = 10 km:

  • With sound waves (cmax = 350 m/s): Δt ≤ 10,000/350 ≈ 29 seconds
  • Without sound waves (cmax = 15 m/s): Δt ≤ 10,000/15 ≈ 667 seconds ≈ 11 minutes

That's a factor of 23× more time steps needed just because of sound waves! Each time step costs computational resources. For a week-long forecast, this means 23× more computation for absolutely no weather benefit.

Concept 27

Method 1 — Complete Elimination by Assuming Incompressibility

The most straightforward approach: declare the atmosphere to be incompressible. If air can't be compressed, sound waves can't exist.

Mathematically, set dρ/dt = 0:

dρ/dt + ρ∇·V = 0   ⇒   ∇·V = 0

The problem: This requires three conditions:

  1. u² ≪ cs² — easily satisfied
  2. Time scales much larger than acoustic propagation time — easily satisfied
  3. Lz ≪ Hz (vertical scale much less than scale height ~8 km) — NOT satisfied for deep circulations!

The third condition is the killer. Sea breezes, mountain waves, and thunderstorms all have deep vertical circulations. The incompressible assumption is too restrictive — it only works for very shallow phenomena.

Concept 28

The Anelastic Approximation — The Practical Solution

The clever compromise: allow density to vary with height but not locally in time.

Anelastic Continuity Equation∇·(ρ₀ V) = 0

This is the anelastic continuity equation.

Why It Works

The full continuity equation allows dρ/dt ≠ 0, which is what sound waves need (local compression/decompression). By forcing ∂ρ/∂t = 0 while keeping ρ₀(z), we:

  • Remove the mechanism that supports sound waves (local compression)
  • Keep the important effect that air thins with altitude
  • Handle deep circulations properly

This is widely used in atmospheric models — the standard approach for mesoscale models needing deep convection without CFL constraints from sound waves.

Concept 29

Alternative Approaches to Handling Sound Waves

Beyond the anelastic approximation: time splitting, artificial slowing, and filtering techniques.

Method 2 — Time Splitting

Integrate fast sound wave modes with a very small time step (or implicit/damping method), while integrating slower gravity and Rossby modes with a larger time step.

  • Advantage: Relatively economical
  • Disadvantage: Complicates code, can interfere with physics

Method 3 — Artificially Slow Down Sound Waves

Modify the equations to reduce the speed of sound (e.g., from 350 to 50 m/s).

  • Advantage: Efficient and straightforward. Most commonly used in practice.
  • Disadvantage: Slightly wrong physics

Method 4 — Artificially Speed Up Sound Waves

Make sound waves infinitely fast (instantaneous pressure adjustment).

  • Advantage: Eliminates sound waves completely
  • Disadvantage: Difficult to implement

Filtering Methods

  • Vertical filters: Semi-implicit treatment of vertical momentum that slows gravity/sound wave growth
  • Temporal filters: Adding diffusion-like correction terms that smooth fast modes over time (temporal smoothing based on current and two stored values)

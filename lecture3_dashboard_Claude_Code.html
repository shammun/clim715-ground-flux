Concept 01

Taylor Series — The Foundation of Everything

The mathematical bridge that converts calculus into arithmetic — the single most important tool in numerical modeling.

Why do we need Taylor series?

In weather and climate modeling, we need to predict the future. The laws of physics give us equations with derivatives (rates of change), but computers cannot do calculus. They can only add, subtract, multiply, and divide. The Taylor series converts derivatives into arithmetic — it lets us approximate any smooth function using only basic operations.

Road analogy:

You're standing at a point on a curvy road. You know your position (the function value) and which direction the road goes (the derivative). You can guess where the road will be a short distance ahead by extrapolating along the slope. Add curvature information, and the guess improves. That's the Taylor series — reconstructing a function from local information.

The formula

f(x) = f(x₀) + f'(x₀)(x − x₀) + f''(x₀)(x − x₀)² / 2! + f'''(x₀)(x − x₀)³ / 3! + ⋯

In compact notation:

f(x) = Σk=0∞ f(k)(x₀) · (x − x₀)k / k!

Building it up term by term

0th order (just the value): f(x) ≈ f(x₀). A flat horizontal line — terrible everywhere except exactly at x₀.

1st order (add slope): f(x) ≈ f(x₀) + f'(x₀)·(x − x₀). The tangent line — good very close to x₀.

2nd order (add curvature): Adds a parabolic bend. For sin(x) at x₀ = 0, the second derivative is zero, so this doesn't help.

3rd order: f(x) ≈ x − x³/6. Now the approximation curves and follows sin(x) much further.

Each term has three pieces

f(k)(x₀)

The k-th derivative evaluated at x₀ — captures the shape of the function at your known point.

(x − x₀)k

Distance raised to the k-th power — grows fast as you move away from x₀.

1 / k!

The factorial shrinks rapidly: 1, 1, ½, ⅙, 1/24, 1/120… This is why the series converges.

Convergence: why truncation works

With infinite terms, the Taylor series is exact. But computers can't sum infinitely many terms. We truncate. The factorial in the denominator makes each term contribute less: the 10th term is 3,628,800× smaller than the first. This rapid shrinkage — convergence — justifies stopping after a few terms.

The punchline for modeling: Future temperature ≈ current temperature + (rate of change) × (time step). That's the truncated Taylor series: T(t + Δt) ≈ T(t) + (dT/dt)·Δt. Every time-stepping scheme in this course is built from this idea.

Worked example: polynomial

f(x) = 4x³ + 2x² − 5x + 1, expanded about x₀ = 2:

f(2) = 31, f'(2) = 51, f''(2) = 52, f'''(2) = 24, f''''(2) = 0.

f(x) = 31 + 51(x−2) + 26(x−2)² + 4(x−2)³

Since f is a cubic, all derivatives of order 4+ are zero — the Taylor series terminates exactly. No truncation error. This is a special case; for sin, exp, etc., the series is infinite.

Concept 02

The Mean Value Theorem

Even a crude one-term truncation gives an exact derivative — if you evaluate it at exactly the right (unknown) point.

From Taylor series to MVT

Truncate to first order: f(x) ≈ f(x₀) + f'(x₀)(x − x₀). This is approximate. But MVT says: there exists a special point x* between x₀ and x where:

f(x) = f(x₀) + f'(x*)(x − x₀)     ← exact!

No truncation error. The catch: we don't know where x* is — just that it exists.

Driving analogy:

Drive 25 miles in 30 minutes → average speed = 50 mph. MVT guarantees that at some moment during the drive, your speedometer read exactly 50 mph.

Rearranging gives the derivative

f'(x*) = [f(x) − f(x₀)] / (x − x₀)

This is the finite difference formula — the ratio of function-value difference to position difference. MVT tells us it equals the exact derivative at some intermediate point x*. This is the theoretical backbone of finite differencing.

The generalized MVT

Keep any number of terms; if the last derivative is evaluated at the right intermediate point, the formula becomes exact. This reveals the form of the truncation error.

Self-check

f(x) = x² on [1, 3]. Secant slope = (9−1)/2 = 4. f'(x) = 2x → need 2x* = 4 → x* = 2. Indeed, x* = 2 is between 1 and 3.

Concept 03

Definition of the Derivative

The derivative IS a finite difference — MVT makes this rigorous. But computers can never take Δx → 0.

From MVT: f'(x*) = [f(x₀+Δx) − f(x₀)] / Δx is exact at the unknown x*. As an approximation at x₀:

f'(x₀) ≈ [f(x₀+Δx) − f(x₀)] / Δx

In the limit: f'(x₀) = limΔx→0 [f(x₀+Δx) − f(x₀)] / Δx. But computers cannot take limits — we're always stuck with finite Δx and therefore always working with an approximation.

Concept 04

The Basis of Modeling

Switch from space to time: the Taylor series becomes the time-stepping engine of every weather model ever built.

The fundamental formula

Replace x with t in the truncated Taylor series:

Future ≈ Present + Rate of Change × Time Step
f(t₂) ≈ f(t₁) + f'(t₁) · Δt

Euler Forward Time-Stepping

1.50
1
True temperatureNumerical predictionTangent (current rate)

Try: 1 step with small Δt (accurate) vs large Δt (overshoot). Then increase steps — errors accumulate over time.

Step 1: Know the present

At t₁ = 0, measure T = 20°C. This is your initial condition.

Step 2: Physics gives tendency

Governing equations yield dT/dt = +3.5 °C/hr. This is the rate of change.

Step 3: Extrapolate

T(t₂) ≈ 20 + 3.5 × 1 = 23.5°C. This is prediction via Taylor series.

Three critical points

1. Derivative at present time: f'(t₁) is used, not f'(t₂). This makes the scheme explicit — meaning the future value is computed directly from known present values, with no need to solve equations. The alternative, an implicit scheme, would use f'(t₂) — but we don't know the future yet, so that requires solving a system of equations (more expensive, but often more stable).

2. Extrapolation involves error: Extrapolation means projecting beyond your known data — guessing about a region where you have no measurements. This is riskier than interpolation (estimating between known points). The error from extrapolation is proportional to (Δt)². Halving the time step reduces per-step error by ~4×.

3. You need governing equations: Taylor series tells you how to step; physics tells you what f'(t₁) is: ∂T/∂t = −V·∇T + ∇²KT + S.

The modeling recipe: (1) Start with ICs → (2) Compute tendencies from governing equations → (3) Step forward: future = present + tendency × Δt → (4) Repeat. Every scheme in this course is a more clever version of step 3.

Concept 05

Eulerian & Lagrangian Viewpoints

Two ways to watch a moving fluid — their difference produces the advection term, the most important term in atmospheric equations.

E

Eulerian (Stand on bridge)

Fixed thermometer in the river. Different water flows past your sensor. Temperature changes because (a) different-temperature water arrives, and (b) the water itself may be heating/cooling.

Notation
∂T/∂t — partial derivative, fixed location
L

Lagrangian (Float in kayak)

Thermometer drifts with the current, always measuring the same parcel. Temperature changes only from physical heating/cooling — not from different water arriving.

Notation
dT/dt — total derivative, following parcel

The chain rule connects them

Since T depends on x, y, z, and t, and the parcel moves (dx/dt = u, dy/dt = v, dz/dt = w):

dT/dt = ∂T/∂t + u·∂T/∂x + v·∂T/∂y + w·∂T/∂z
dT/dt = ∂T/∂t + V · ∇T

The term V·∇T is advection — how much temperature at a fixed point changes because wind blows different-temperature air in.

Concrete example: cold front

Winter day. Northerly wind at 10 m/s. Air to the north is 5°C colder per 100 km.

Advection: v·∂T/∂y = (−10)(+5×10−5) ≈ −1.8 °C/hour (cold advection)

Lagrangian: Radiative cooling at −0.5°C/hour

Eulerian total: ∂T/∂t = −0.5 − 1.8 = −2.3 °C/hour

Key operator identity: d/dt = ∂/∂t + V·∇. This appears in every governing equation. The general prognostic equation ∂ψ/∂t = −V·∇ψ + ∇²Kψ + S has advection as the conversion factor between viewpoints.

Concept 06

The Hydrostatic Approximation

The vertical pressure gradient (~976 cm/s²) and gravity (~980 cm/s²) nearly perfectly cancel. This simplifies the vertical momentum equation dramatically.

The hydrostatic equation

dp/dz = −ρg

Pressure decreases with height at a rate equal to density times gravity. This represents the balance between the upward pressure gradient force and the downward pull of gravity.

What does the approximation do?

Without: Solve dw/dt = −(1/ρ)∂p/∂z − g. This is a prognostic equation — it predicts how vertical velocity evolves over time, requiring computation at every time step. Expensive, and supports fast acoustic waves that force tiny time steps.

With: Replace with (1/ρ)∂p/∂z = −g. This is a diagnostic equation — it tells you the relationship between pressure and height at any instant, with no time derivative. No time stepping needed, no acoustic waves. Much cheaper.

Prognostic vs. diagnostic:

A prognostic equation is like a recipe: "Start with X, do this each minute, and you'll get Y after an hour." It marches forward in time step by step.

A diagnostic equation is like a formula: "Given X, Y must be this." It's a snapshot — no history, no time stepping. Just an instant relationship.

When does it break down?

Valid when Lx ≫ Lz (synoptic-scale, global models). Fails for: thunderstorm updrafts (~30 m/s), sea-breeze fronts, turbulent eddies → need non-hydrostatic models (WRF).

Concept 07

The Boussinesq Approximation

Density perturbations are tiny (<1-2%) — ignore them everywhere except in the buoyancy term, where gravity amplifies them into convection.

Split variables into reference + perturbation

ρ(x,y,z,t) = ρ₀(z) + ρ'(x,y,z,t)     where ρ' ≪ ρ₀

What is a "perturbation"?

Think of the average ocean depth (the reference state ρ₀) versus the waves on top (the perturbation ρ'). The waves are tiny compared to the total depth, but they're the interesting part — they carry energy and do things. Similarly, density perturbations are small departures from the average, but they drive weather.

Rule 1: Replace ρ with ρ₀ everywhere

In the pressure gradient terms (1/ρ factor), using ρ₀ instead of ρ introduces <1% error — negligible.

Rule 2: KEEP ρ' in buoyancy

The buoyancy term is the force that makes warm air rise and cold air sink. It's the term where density difference is multiplied by gravity: −(ρ'/ρ₀)g. Even a 0.5% density difference, amplified by g = 9.8 m/s², produces enough acceleration to build thermals and thunderstorms.

The temperature-density connection

ρ'/ρ ≈ −θ'/θ

Warm parcel (θ' > 0) → less dense (ρ' < 0) → rises. Cold parcel → denser → sinks. Density differences can be computed from temperature alone!

Valid for: Shallow layers (PBL), ocean modeling, lab convection.

Fails for: Deep convection spanning the troposphere. Use anelastic instead.

Concept 08

Potential Temperature (θ)

A temperature-like variable that only changes when heat is physically added or removed — immune to "trivial" pressure changes.

The problem with regular temperature

Lift a parcel 3 km → T drops ~30°C. No heat was exchanged — it cooled simply by expanding at lower pressure. This "trivial" cooling makes T a poor tracer of actual heating/cooling.

Definition

θ = T · (p₀/p)Rd/cpd     where p₀ = 1000 mb, Rd/cpd ≈ 0.286

θ is the temperature a parcel would have if brought adiabatically to 1000 mb.

Lapse rate = how fast temperature drops with altitude in the environment. The standard atmosphere is ~6.5°C/km. The dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR) is always 9.8°C/km — this is the rate at which a rising parcel cools due to expansion alone.

Why θ is conserved

When a parcel rises: T decreases (expansion), (p₀/p)κ increases (lower p). These exactly cancel — by design.

Adiabatic vs. diabatic: the two types of processes

Adiabatic process ("no heat crossing the boundary")

The parcel changes temperature purely from being compressed or expanded by surrounding pressure — like a bicycle pump getting warm when you squeeze it, or a spray can getting cold when gas escapes. No heat enters or leaves the parcel from the outside. θ is conserved (unchanged) during adiabatic processes.

Diabatic process ("heat crosses the boundary")

The parcel actually gains or loses heat from an external source. These are the processes that do change θ:

→ Radiation: The parcel absorbs sunlight or emits infrared heat to space

→ Latent heating: Water vapor condenses inside the parcel, releasing stored heat (this is why thunderstorms are so energetic)

→ Turbulent mixing: The parcel mixes with neighboring air at a different temperature

→ Surface contact: Air touching warm ground heats up; air touching cold snow cools down

In summary: θ changes only during diabatic processes. If none of these are happening (purely adiabatic motion), dθ/dt = 0 — θ is perfectly conserved, no matter how far the parcel travels vertically.

Why models use θ instead of T

Simpler equations

dθ/dt = diabatic terms only. If no heating: dθ/dt = 0. The big "trivial" adiabatic terms vanish completely.

Direct stability

dθ/dz > 0: stable (displaced parcel returns — like a ball in a bowl). dθ/dz = 0: neutral (parcel stays wherever moved). dθ/dz < 0: unstable (displaced parcel accelerates away — triggers convection).

Conservation

θ is conserved in adiabatic flow — if a model produces spurious θ changes, you know there's a numerical error.

Concept 09

The Exner Function (π)

A non-dimensional pressure that pairs with θ to eliminate density from the hydrostatic equation.

Definition

π = (p / p₀)Rd/cpd

Non-dimensional means π has no units — it's just a pure number between 0 and 1. This is because we divided pressure by the reference pressure (mb ÷ mb = unitless), then raised it to a power. Working with unitless numbers simplifies the math and improves numerical stability.

The beautiful relationship

T = θ · π

Temperature = potential temperature × Exner function. π encodes all pressure information needed to convert between T and θ.

The payoff: simplified hydrostatic equation

Original: dp/dz = −ρg (three variables: p, ρ, z, needs equation of state).

After substituting π and T = θ·π, density cancels:

dπ/dz = −g / (cpd · θv)

Only two variables (π, θv) plus constants. Density eliminated! Numerically cleaner because π varies gently (0→1) compared to p's exponential decay.

Virtual potential temperature

θv = θ(1 + 0.61·qv)

The virtual temperature is a trick to account for moisture without rewriting the ideal gas law. Moist air is actually lighter than dry air at the same temperature — because water vapor molecules (H₂O, molecular weight 18) are lighter than nitrogen (N₂ = 28) and oxygen (O₂ = 32). The 0.61·qv correction bumps up θ slightly in moist air to reflect this lower density. In dry air (qv = 0), θv = θ exactly.

Concept 10

The Continuity Equation

Conservation of mass — air cannot be created or destroyed. Connects winds to density changes.

Lagrangian form

dρ/dt + ρ · ∇·V = 0

The symbol ∇·V is called divergence — it measures whether the flow is spreading out (positive, like air blowing away from a point) or converging (negative, like air flowing toward a low-pressure center). If flow diverges (∇·V > 0): parcel expands, density decreases. If flow converges: parcel is compressed, density increases.

Eulerian (flux) form

∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρV) = 0

Density at a fixed point changes when mass flux in ≠ mass flux out.

The incompressible limit

If dρ/dt = 0: ∇·V = 0. Whatever flows in must flow out. Valid when u² ≪ cs², and Lz ≪ scale height (~8.5 km). The scale height is the altitude over which atmospheric pressure drops by a factor of e (≈2.718) — roughly 8.5 km. If your circulation is much shallower than this (e.g., a boundary layer process at 1 km), the incompressible assumption works. But many weather systems span 10+ km, violating this condition.

Concept 11

The Anelastic Approximation

The sweet spot — density varies with height but not in time. Filters sound waves, allows deep circulations, enables large time steps.

∇ · (ρ₀ V) = 0

Density varies with height through ρ₀(z) but ∂ρ/∂t = 0. Sound waves (which need density oscillations) are eliminated.

Why it filters sound waves

What does "filter" mean here? It means the equation is mathematically incapable of producing sound waves — like removing a piano key so that note can never be played. Sound waves are compression waves — they work by alternately squeezing and stretching air at a fixed location, which requires density to oscillate in time (∂ρ/∂t ≠ 0). By setting ∂ρ/∂t = 0, we've removed the restoring mechanism that sound needs. Gravity waves and Rossby waves survive because they work through buoyancy and vorticity, not local density oscillations.

7× speedup: Without sound waves, the CFL limit is set by wind/gravity wave speed (~50 m/s) instead of sound speed (~350 m/s). This allows ~7× larger time steps — the difference between a 1-day and a 1-week computation.

Concept 12

The Scalar Equation

The universal template — every prognostic variable in every atmospheric model obeys this equation. Only the source term changes.

The Scalar Transport Equation ∂c/∂t  +  V·∇c  =  ∇·(κ∇c)  +  S

∂c/∂t — Eulerian tendency

Rate of change at a fixed grid point. Everything on the right causes this change.

V·∇c — Advection

Wind carries the scalar. Preserves total amount. Hyperbolic. Hardest term numerically.

∇·(κ∇c) — Diffusion

Turbulent mixing. Smooths gradients, like dropping ink in water — it spreads from concentrated to dilute. Parabolic and irreversible: once mixed, you can't un-mix. The eddy diffusivity κe ≈ 1–100 m²/s is about a million times larger than molecular diffusivity, because turbulent eddies mix air far more efficiently than individual molecules bouncing around.

S — Sources/sinks

Everything that creates or destroys the quantity: radiation heating, condensation releasing latent heat, factory emissions, chemical reactions, rain washing pollutants out. This term is where parameterizations live — approximate formulas that represent complex physical processes the model can't resolve directly.

Mathematical nature: mixed hyperbolic-parabolic

Advection (V·∇c) is hyperbolic — information travels along specific paths (wind trajectories) at a definite speed, like a message being carried downstream. Diffusion (∇·(κ∇c)) is parabolic — information spreads in all directions simultaneously, smoothing everything out and gradually "forgetting" what the initial condition looked like (like perfume diffusing in a room — eventually it's uniform everywhere). In the free atmosphere (small κ), advection dominates and features stay sharp. In the PBL (large κ), diffusion dominates and everything gets smoothed out.

Universal template: Every prognostic variable — T, q, u, v, ozone, pollutants — follows ∂ψ/∂t = −V·∇ψ + ∇²Kψ + S. The rest of this course is about solving this equation numerically: finite differences (Lectures 5–7), time stepping (Lecture 6), stability (Lecture 5), and turbulence parameterization (Lecture 9).

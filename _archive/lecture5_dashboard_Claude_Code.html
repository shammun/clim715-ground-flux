Concept 01

What Are Finite Differences?

Approximating derivatives using nearby grid-point values — the foundation of numerical weather prediction.

The Core Problem

We have equations that describe the atmosphere, like:

1D Advection Equation∂u/∂t + c · ∂u/∂x = 0

This equation says: "the wind speed u changes over time because it's being carried (advected) by a flow moving at speed c." The symbols ∂u/∂t and ∂u/∂x are derivatives — they describe how something changes continuously, at every single point in space and time.

The problem: Computers cannot do calculus. They can add, subtract, multiply, and divide numbers — but they cannot compute a true derivative, because a derivative requires knowing the function at infinitely many points infinitely close together. A computer only stores a finite number of values at specific locations.

The solution: We approximate the derivative using values at a few nearby points. This approximation is called a finite difference.

Analogy: Imagine you're driving on a highway and your speedometer is broken. You can still estimate your speed by noting your position at two different times:

speed ≈ (position at time 2 − position at time 1) / (time 2 − time 1)

That's a finite difference! You're replacing the instantaneous rate of change (what the speedometer shows) with an average rate of change over a small interval.

Two Ways to Represent Functions on a Computer

There are two fundamentally different ways to store a continuous function on a computer:

Way 1 — Grid Points (Finite Differences)

You pick specific locations in space (x1, x2, x3, ...) and store the value of the function at each point. Like recording the temperature at weather stations scattered across a state.

  • What is stored: Values fi = f(xi)
  • Pros: Easy to implement, local stencils (uses adjacent grid points)
  • Cons: Lower accuracy unless grid is very fine

Way 2 — Series Expansion (Spectral Methods)

You store coefficients of mathematical basis functions (like sine and cosine waves) that, when added together, reconstruct the function. Like describing a musical chord by listing the frequencies and volumes of each note.

  • What is stored: Coefficients ak
  • Pros: Very high accuracy for smooth problems
  • Cons: Global basis (spans entire domain) — harder with shocks and boundaries

This course focuses on Way 1 — the grid-point / finite-difference approach — because it's the most widely used method in weather and climate models, and because time is almost always treated with finite differences even in spectral models.

The Three Basic Finite Difference Formulas

The derivative of a function f at point x0 is defined as:

Definition of the Derivativef'(x0) = limΔx→0 [f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0)] / Δx

This says: "take two points that are Δx apart, compute the slope between them, and then let Δx shrink to zero." In the limit, you get the exact derivative.

But on a computer, Δx can never be zero — our grid points are separated by some finite spacing. So we drop the limit and just use the fraction with a finite Δx. This gives three different approximations depending on which points we use:

1. Forward (Right-Sided) Difference

f'(x0) ≈ [f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0)] / Δx

You look forward (to the right) from x0.

2. Backward (Left-Sided) Difference

f'(x0) ≈ [f(x0) − f(x0 − Δx)] / Δx

You look backward (to the left) from x0.

3. Centered (Leapfrog) Difference

f'(x0) ≈ [f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0 − Δx)] / (2Δx)

You look both ways from x0, straddling it symmetrically.

Key point: If the derivative is continuous and Δx → 0, all three give the same answer — the true derivative. But when Δx is finite (which it always is on a computer), they are not equivalent. They differ in their accuracy and structure, and these differences profoundly affect the stability of the numerical solution.

Concept 02

Accuracy and Truncation Error

How good are these finite difference approximations? Taylor series reveals the exact error.

The Big Idea

We want to answer: How good are these finite difference approximations? How big is the error?

The strategy:

  1. We have a finite difference formula (like the forward difference)
  2. We want to know the exact relationship between this formula and the true derivative
  3. We use Taylor series to expand the function values in the formula, revealing exactly what the formula computes — the true derivative PLUS some extra "error" terms
  4. Those extra terms are the truncation error — the price we pay for using a finite Δx

Taylor Series Review

If you know everything about a function at one point x0 (its value, its first derivative, its second derivative, etc.), you can reconstruct the function's value at any nearby point x0 + Δx:

Taylor Series Expansionf(x0 + Δx) = f(x0) + Δx · f'(x0) + (Δx)²/2! · f''(x0) + (Δx)³/3! · f'''(x0) + …

Breaking down every piece:

  • f(x0) is the function value at your "starting point"
  • f'(x0) is the first derivative at x0 — the slope
  • f''(x0) is the second derivative — the curvature
  • Δx is the distance you're stepping away from x0
  • 2! = 2 × 1 = 2, 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 (factorials)

Each successive term involves a higher power of Δx. If Δx is small (say, 0.01), then (Δx)² = 0.0001 and (Δx)³ = 0.000001 — each term becomes much smaller than the previous one. This is why truncating (cutting off) the series after a few terms still gives a good approximation.

Applying Taylor Series to the Forward Difference

1

Write the Taylor expansion for f(x0 + Δx)

f(x0 + Δx) = f(x0) + Δx · f'(x0) + (Δx)²/2! · f''(x0) + (Δx)³/3! · f'''(x0) + …
2

Subtract f(x0) from both sides

f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0) = Δx · f'(x0) + (Δx)²/2! · f''(x0) + (Δx)³/3! · f'''(x0) + …

The f(x0) terms cancel completely.

3

Divide everything by Δx

[f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0)] / Δx = f'(x0) + (Δx/2!) · f''(x0) + (Δx)²/3! · f'''(x0) + …

Result: The forward difference equals:

Forward Difference = True Derivative + Truncation Error f'(x0) + Δx/2 · f''(x0) + (Δx)²/6 · f'''(x0) + …

What Is "Truncation Error"?

The truncation error is everything beyond the true derivative — all those extra terms that exist because Δx is not zero. Written compactly:

[f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0)] / Δx = f'(x0) + O(Δx)

The notation O(Δx) — "order Δx" or "big-O of Δx" — means the leading error term is proportional to Δx to the first power.

Order of Accuracy: Definition and Significance

The order of accuracy is determined by the lowest power of Δx in the truncation error:

Leading error proportional toOrder of accuracyEffect of halving Δx
Δx¹First-orderError cut in half (2× better)
Δx²Second-orderError cut to one quarter (4× better)
Δx³Third-orderError cut to one eighth (8× better)

Higher-order schemes give much more "bang for your buck" when increasing resolution.

The forward difference is first-order accurate (leading error ~ Δx¹).

Taylor Series Analysis of the Centered Difference

The centered difference formula is:

[f(x0 + Δx) − f(x0 − Δx)] / (2Δx)

We need Taylor expansions in both directions.

Taylor Series Cancellation in Centered Differences

Step 1: Write both Taylor expansions

f(x0 + Δx) =

f + Δx·f' + Δx²/2·f'' + Δx³/6·f''' + Δx⁴/24·f''''

f(x0 − Δx) =

f − Δx·f' + Δx²/2·f'' − Δx³/6·f''' + Δx⁴/24·f''''

Odd-power terms flip sign between rows; even-power terms keep the same sign.

Step 2: Subtract bottom from top

0 + 2Δx·f' 0 + 2Δx³/6·f''' 0

Even-power terms cancelled perfectly. Only odd-power terms survive and double!

Step 3: Divide by 2Δx

f'(x0) + (Δx²/6)·f'''(x0) + … O(Δx²) — SECOND-ORDER ACCURATE!

Concept 03

The Upstream (Upwind) Scheme — A Complete Case Study

Applying finite differences to the 1D advection equation reveals the fundamental trade-off between accuracy and stability.

The Physical Problem: 1D Advection

We solve:

1D Advection Equation∂u/∂t + c · ∂u/∂x = 0

Physical meaning: A quantity u (temperature, moisture, pollutant) is carried along by a constant wind speed c, without any change in shape or amplitude. Imagine a blob of dye floating downstream in a river — it doesn't dissolve or spread, it just translates at the river's speed.

Exact solution: u(x, t) = f(x − ct) — the initial shape f simply shifts to the right at speed c, perfectly preserved. This makes it an ideal test case: we know the exact answer.

Discretizing: The Space-Time Grid

In space: Grid points at regular intervals: xj = j · Δx, for j = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

In time: Discrete time steps: tn = n · Δt, for n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...

The value of u at grid point j and time level n is written: ujn ≡ u(jΔx, nΔt)

The subscript tells you where in space, the superscript tells you when in time.

The Upstream Scheme Stencil

Upstream Scheme as Weighted Average — Interactive

0.50
Weight on upstream (j−1) Weight on current (j)
μ = 0.50: Future value = 50% of current point + 50% of upstream point. The wave moves half a grid cell per step.

Building the Upstream Scheme

Time derivative — forward difference in time:

∂u/∂t ≈ (ujn+1 − ujn) / Δt

Space derivative — backward difference in space (looking upstream, left when c > 0):

∂u/∂x ≈ (ujn − uj−1n) / Δx

Combined:

(ujn+1 − ujn) / Δt + c · (ujn − uj−1n) / Δx = 0

Solving for the unknown:

ujn+1 = ujn − (cΔt/Δx) · (ujn − uj−1n)

The Courant Number μ

Courant Numberμ = cΔt / Δx

Physical meaning: The ratio of the distance the wave travels in one time step (cΔt) to the grid spacing (Δx):

  • μ = 1: wave moves exactly one grid cell per step
  • μ = 0.5: wave moves half a grid cell per step
  • μ = 2: wave moves two grid cells per step (will cause trouble!)

Using μ, the upstream scheme becomes:

Upstream Scheme (Compact Form)ujn+1 = (1 − μ) · ujn + μ · uj−1n

Interpretation: The future value is a weighted average of the present value at point j and the upstream value at j−1, with weights determined by μ. When μ = 0, nothing moves. When μ = 1, the value shifts exactly one cell upstream — physically perfect.

Truncation Error of the Full Scheme

Using Taylor series to expand ujn+1 and uj−1n around (j, n), substituting into the scheme, and simplifying:

ut + c·ux = −(Δt/2)utt + (cΔx/2)uxx − (Δt)²/6 · uttt − c(Δx)²/6 · uxxx + …

The left side is the PDE we want (should equal zero). The right side is the truncation error — nonzero for finite Δt, Δx.

The Modified Equation: Discovering Numerical Diffusion

After algebraic manipulation (replacing utt using the PDE repeatedly), the truncation error simplifies to:

Modified Equation — The Most Important Resultut + c·ux = (cΔx/2)(1 − cΔt/Δx) · uxx + O[(Δt)², (Δx)²]

This is the most important result of this analysis. The right side has a uxx term — the diffusion operator! The scheme is NOT solving pure advection. It's solving advection + artificial diffusion, with numerical diffusion coefficient:

Numerical Diffusion CoefficientKnumerical = (cΔx/2)(1 − μ)

This numerical diffusion:

  • Is NOT real physics — it's an artifact of the scheme
  • Causes the wave amplitude to decrease each time step (except at μ = 1)
  • Over long integrations, it diffuses away all features — fronts, gradients, boundaries get smoothed
  • Makes the upstream scheme useless for long-term integrations like climate simulations

Special case μ = 1: Knumerical = (cΔx/2)(1 − 1) = 0 — diffusion vanishes! The wave moves exactly one grid cell per step, so no interpolation smearing occurs.

Concept 04

Consistency — "Are We Solving the Right Equation?"

Consistency ensures that as the grid is refined, the numerical scheme actually reduces to the original PDE.

Definition

Consistency asks: If we make our grid infinitely fine (Δx → 0 and Δt → 0), does our numerical scheme actually reduce to the original PDE we're trying to solve?

  • If yes → the scheme is consistent.
  • If no → no matter how fine we make the grid, we'll never approach the correct answer — we'd be solving a different equation.

Checking Consistency for the Upstream Scheme

The truncation error is:

τ = −(Δt/2)utt + (cΔx/2)uxx − (Δt)²/6 · uttt − c(Δx)²/6 · uxxx + …

Every term in τ contains at least one power of Δt or Δx. Therefore:

Consistency Checkτ → 0 as Δt → 0 and Δx → 0

The upstream scheme is consistent. In the limit of infinitely fine resolution, the numerical equation collapses back to the true PDE.

Can a Scheme Fail Consistency?

Yes. If Δt and Δx are linked in an unusual way that prevents certain error terms from vanishing. For example, a truncation error term like (Δt)²/Δx would not vanish if Δt = (Δx)1/2, because (Δt)²/Δx = Δx/Δx = 1 ≠ 0.

For the upstream scheme, no such pathological relationship is needed.

Consistency Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Consistency alone does NOT guarantee a good numerical solution. It only guarantees you're solving the right equation. The solution could still blow up if the scheme is unstable.

Analogy: Consistency is like verifying you have the correct recipe for a cake. But even with the correct recipe, you could ruin the cake if your oven temperature is wildly unstable. You need both the right recipe (consistency) AND a stable oven (stability) to get a good cake (convergence).

The Lax Equivalence Theorem (Lax & Richtmyer, 1956)

For linear, well-posed initial value problems:

Lax Equivalence TheoremConsistency + Stability ⇔ Convergence

The double arrow means:

  • Convergence happens if and only if the scheme is both consistent AND stable
  • Consistency alone is NOT enough — a consistent but unstable scheme will blow up
  • Stability alone is NOT enough — a stable but inconsistent scheme converges to the wrong answer
  • You need BOTH to guarantee convergence

Concept 05

Stability — "Will the Solution Blow Up?"

Stability ensures the numerical solution stays bounded. The Energy Method provides one way to prove it.

The Problem We're Worried About

Imagine running a weather model. After 100 steps, temperatures look fine. After 500 steps, small wiggles appear. After 1000 steps, enormous oscillations. After 2000 steps, a grid point reads 10,000°C. The model has blown up. This is numerical instability — a purely numerical disease, unrelated to physics.

The Precise Definition

Setup: Let φjn = numerical solution, ψ(nΔt, jΔx) = true solution.

Formal statement: A scheme is stable if:

Stability Definition‖φn‖ ≤ CT · ‖φ0‖ for all nΔt ≤ T

Where:

  • ‖φn‖ = the norm (measure of "size") of the solution at time step n
  • ‖φ0‖ = the norm of the initial condition
  • CT = a constant that may depend on final time T, but NOT on Δx or Δt

In plain English: The solution at any future time cannot be more than CT times bigger than the initial condition.

What Is a Norm?

A norm measures the "size" or "magnitude" of a collection of numbers on the grid.

Maximum norm (ℓ∞)

‖φ‖∞ = maxj |φj|

Finds the largest value anywhere on the grid. Like asking: "What's the worst-case temperature error at any single station?"

Example: vector (3, −7, 2) → ℓ∞ norm = |−7| = 7.

Euclidean norm (ℓ2)

‖φ‖2 = (Σj |φj|² Δx)1/2

Measures "total energy" across the grid. Connected to physical quantities like total kinetic energy.

Example: vector (3, −7, 2) with Δx=1 → ℓ2 norm = (9+49+4)1/2 = 7.87.

What Stability Does NOT Promise

  1. Says nothing about solution quality at finite Δx, Δt — only promises good behavior in the limit
  2. Allows solutions that grow with time — as long as growth doesn't depend on grid size (physical solutions sometimes grow)
  3. For constant-amplitude problems (like advection), simplifies to: ‖φn‖ ≤ ‖φ0‖

The Energy Method

Core idea: Track the total "energy" from one time step to the next, show it never grows.

  1. Define discrete energy: En = Σj (φjn)² · Δx
  2. Express En+1 in terms of En using the scheme
  3. Show En+1 ≤ En

Energy Method Applied to the Upstream Scheme: Step-by-Step

Start with: φjn+1 = (1 − μ)φjn + μφj−1n

Energy Method Derivation — 6 Steps

1

Start with the scheme

φjn+1 = (1 − μ)φjn + μφj−1n
2

Square both sides, sum over all j

Σj(φjn+1)² = (1−μ)² Σj(φjn)² + 2μ(1−μ)Σjφjnφj−1n + μ² Σj(φj−1n)²

Using the identity (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²

3

Periodic BCs: shift indices freely

Σj(φj−1n)² = Σj(φjn)²

Shifting all indices by one doesn't change the sum (same numbers, different order).

4

Rearrange into energy form

Using (1−μ)² + μ² = 1 − 2μ(1−μ):

En+1 = En − 2μ(1−μ)[Σj(φjn)² − Σjφjnφj−1n]
5

Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

Σjφjnφj−1n ≤ (Σj(φjn)²)1/2 · (Σj(φj−1n)²)1/2 = Σj(φjn)²

The "cross product" sum ≤ the "self product" sum. So the bracket […] is non-negative (≥ 0).

6

Determine stability condition

For En+1 ≤ En, the subtracted term must be non-negative. Since the bracket ≥ 0, we need:

μ(1 − μ) ≥ 0

This requires: μ ≥ 0 (automatic) AND (1 − μ) ≥ 0 (i.e., μ ≤ 1).

Concept 06

Von Neumann's Stability Method

A systematic, mechanical procedure for analyzing stability of any linear, constant-coefficient scheme.

Why Von Neumann?

The Energy Method requires problem-specific creativity. Von Neumann's Method provides a systematic, mechanical procedure for any linear, constant-coefficient finite difference scheme.

Musical analogy: Think of the numerical solution as a musical chord — a superposition of "notes" (Fourier modes) of different frequencies. Von Neumann asks: Does each individual "note" grow or decay after one time step?

If every note stays the same or shrinks → stable. If any single note grows → that note eventually dominates → unstable.

Step 1: Fourier Decomposition

Any function on the grid can be written as:

φjn = Σk akn · eikjΔx

Where:

  • eikjΔx = a single Fourier mode with wavenumber k (one "note")
  • akn = amplitude of that mode at time n (how "loud")
  • Sum goes over all resolvable wavenumbers, N = π/Δx

Critical insight: For linear constant-coefficient schemes, each mode evolves independently. We analyze one mode at a time: φjn = akn · eikjΔx

Step 2: The Amplification Factor

After one time step:

Ak = akn+1 / akn

After n steps:

akn = (Ak)n · ak0

|Ak| > 1

Mode amplifies → (Ak)n → ∞ → blow-up!

|Ak| = 1

Mode unchanged → neutral (perfect)

|Ak| < 1

Mode shrinks → (Ak)n → 0 → damping

Step 3: The Von Neumann Stability Criterion

Von Neumann Stability Criterion|Ak| ≤ 1 for all resolvable k

If even one wavenumber has |Ak| > 1, that mode eventually dominates and blows up the solution.

Step 4: Computing Ak for the Upstream Scheme

1

Start with the scheme

φjn+1 = (1 − μ)φjn + μφj−1n
2

Substitute Fourier mode

akn+1 eikjΔx = (1−μ) akn eikjΔx + μ akn eik(j−1)Δx

Key: eik(j−1)Δx = eikjΔx · e−ikΔx

3

Divide by akn eikjΔx

Ak = 1 − μ + μ e−ikΔx

Step 5: Finding |Ak|²

Ak is complex. Multiply by its complex conjugate:

|Ak|² = Ak · Ak* = (1−μ + μe−ikΔx)(1−μ + μe+ikΔx)

Two key identities used:

  • Euler's formula: eiθ + e−iθ = 2cosθ
  • Exponential product: eiθ · e−iθ = e0 = 1

Expanding and simplifying:

|Ak|² = (1−μ)² + 2μ(1−μ)cos(kΔx) + μ²

Using (1−μ)² + μ² = 1 − 2μ(1−μ):

Amplification Factor Squared|Ak|² = 1 − 2μ(1−μ)(1 − cos(kΔx))

Analysis of the Result

The term (1 − cos(kΔx)) varies with wavenumber:

  • Long waves (kΔx → 0): (1−cos(kΔx)) ≈ 0, so |Ak|² ≈ 1 — almost no error
  • Short waves (kΔx → π, the 2Δx wave): (1−cosπ) = 2, so |Ak|² = 1 − 4μ(1−μ) — maximum error

The shortest resolvable wave (2Δx) always has the worst amplification factor. When the scheme goes unstable (μ > 1), it's the 2Δx wave that blows up first.

Stability Condition from Von Neumann

For |Ak|² ≤ 1 for all k:

2μ(1−μ)(1 − cos(kΔx)) ≥ 0

Since (1 − cos(kΔx)) ≥ 0 for all resolvable k, we need μ(1−μ) ≥ 0, giving:

CFL Condition (Von Neumann)0 ≤ cΔt / Δx ≤ 1

Same CFL condition as the Energy Method! Both approaches agree.

Universal Lesson: Do not invest much importance into the shortest waves owing to their poor handling. Make sure you choose your grid spacing to properly resolve the phenomenon you are trying to advect. For example, if your primary wave of interest is 10 km wide, choose Δx on the order of 1 km, so your feature is 10Δx wide.

Concept 07

The CFL Condition — Deep Dive and Physical Interpretation

The most important stability condition in computational science, discovered in 1928 — before digital computers existed.

Historical Context

In 1928, Richard Courant, Kurt Friedrichs, and Hans Lewy published their paper — long before digital computers. They were using finite differences to prove existence of PDE solutions, not to build numerical methods. But they discovered a stability condition that became one of the most important results in computational science.

Physical Interpretation: Domain of Dependence

For the advection equation ut + cux = 0, characteristics are straight lines: x − ct = constant. The true solution at any point (x, t) depends only on the initial data where the characteristic intersects t = 0. This is the physical domain of dependence.

A numerical scheme also has a domain of dependence — the set of grid points at previous time levels that influence the computed value. For the upstream scheme, ujn+1 depends on ujn and uj−1n. Tracing back further creates a triangle of influence — the numerical domain of dependence.

The CFL condition states: The numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical domain of dependence. If the characteristic "escapes" outside the numerical stencil, the scheme is trying to compute a solution without access to the needed physical information → instability.

CFL Domain of Dependence — Stable vs. Unstable

The Sampling Rate Interpretation

Define the sampling rate: s = Δx / (cΔt) = 1/μ

  • s = 1 (μ = 1): wave moves one grid point per step — sampled at every point
  • s = 2 (μ = 0.5): wave moves half a grid point — sampled twice per cell
  • s = 0.5 (μ = 2): wave moves two grid points — misses every other point!

When s < 1 (μ > 1), we're "sampling too slowly" — analogous to violating the Nyquist theorem.

Caveat: Making Δt extremely small does NOT automatically improve the solution. Quality depends on the ratio cΔt/Δx, not Δt alone.

Practical Implications for Atmospheric Modeling

The CFL condition imposes a maximum allowed time step: Δt ≤ Δx / c

For the fastest atmospheric wave (sound at ~350 m/s):

Δx = 10 km

Δt ≤ 10,000 / 350 ≈ 29 seconds

Δx = 1 km

Δt ≤ 1,000 / 350 ≈ 3 seconds

Doubling spatial resolution forces halving the time step. Combined with twice as many grid points in each direction, a factor-of-2 refinement in 3D costs roughly 2⁴ = 16 times more computation.

This is why sound waves are a nuisance in atmospheric models — meteorologically unimportant but they dictate the time step.

Critical Caveat: The CFL condition does NOT guarantee stability! It only guarantees instability if violated! CFL is necessary (must satisfy it) but not always sufficient (satisfying it doesn't automatically ensure stability). Example: the forward-time, centered-space (FTCS) scheme satisfies CFL but is unconditionally unstable — its amplification factor always exceeds 1 regardless of μ.

Concept 08

Model Evaluation and Test Cases

How do we know if our numerical scheme works? Standardized tests probe different aspects of performance.

The Fundamental Challenge

Numerical atmospheric modeling is an ill-posed problem:

  • Observations are sparse and contain errors
  • Lateral and top boundary conditions are poorly defined
  • Surface boundary conditions are incompletely specified
  • Motions span many temporal and spatial scales

Because of this, no mathematically rigorous validation of a complete atmospheric modeling system is possible.

The Sub-System Testing Approach

What we can do is validate individual sub-systems:

Sub-systemTested for
Data ingest (QA/QC)Accuracy
Data assimilationRealism
Advection/diffusion operatorsAccuracy
PBL/microphysics/turbulenceRealism

Then the entire system is compared to observations.

The Scalar Advection Test

The most fundamental sub-system test:

Scalar Advection Test∂ψ/∂t + uj · ∂ψ/∂xj = 0

The wind field is prescribed (not computed), isolating the advection scheme's performance from everything else.

Test 1: The Rotating Cone Test (Convergence)

Setup:

  • Wind field: Solid-body rotation — a spinning turntable: u(x,y) = −(y − y0)/xmax, v(x,y) = (x − x0)/ymax
  • Tracer: A cone peaked off-center: ψ(x,y) = max(0, 1 − r/rcone) where r = distance from cone center
  • Mesh: 100 × 100 units; cone at (50, 80) with radius 10 units, peak height 1

The test: Run for exactly one full revolution (360°). The cone should return to its starting position and shape exactly. Any difference is purely numerical error.

What errors look like:

  • Numerical diffusion → cone loses height, broadens (shorter and fatter)
  • Phase errors → cone arrives at the wrong angular position
  • Dispersion errors → trailing oscillations/wiggles behind the cone

Results: A 1st-order scheme exhibits large numerical diffusion after one revolution — the cone is severely flattened. Higher-order schemes preserve the cone much better.

Test 2: Smolarkiewicz's Deformational Flow Test (Stability)

The flow field deforms — stretches and squeezes the tracer. This creates sharp gradients that stress-test the scheme's ability to maintain features without spurious oscillations. Probes nonlinear instability and behavior at unresolved scales.

Test 3: Doswell's Frontogenesis Test (Accuracy/Convergence)

Simulates front formation — very sharp gradients approaching discontinuities. Tests where finite-difference approximations struggle most.

Summary of Everything in Notes #5 Part 2

The Complete Intellectual Arc

  1. Finite differences — how to approximate derivatives on a computer using nearby grid values. Three types: forward, backward, centered.
  2. Taylor series analysis — reveals the exact error (truncation error) in each approximation. Forward/backward are 1st-order accurate, centered is 2nd-order. The order of accuracy tells you how fast errors decrease with grid refinement.
  3. The upstream scheme — a complete case study applying finite differences to the 1D advection equation. Forward-in-time, backward-in-space, explicit. The modified equation reveals its fatal flaw: numerical diffusion that destroys wave amplitudes.
  4. Four pillars of scheme evaluation:
    • Accuracy — how well does the scheme approximate derivatives? (measured by truncation error order)
    • Consistency — does it reduce to the true PDE as Δx, Δt → 0? (check that τ → 0)
    • Stability — does the solution stay bounded? (checked via Energy or Von Neumann method)
    • Convergence — does the numerical solution → true solution? (guaranteed by Lax Equivalence Theorem if consistent + stable)
  5. Two stability analysis methods:
    • Energy Method — define discrete energy, show it doesn't grow. Works for nonlinear problems but requires creativity.
    • Von Neumann Method — decompose into Fourier modes, check each amplification factor |Ak| ≤ 1. Systematic, reveals which wavenumbers are most affected. The workhorse tool.
  6. The CFL condition — 0 ≤ cΔt/Δx ≤ 1. Physical meaning: the wave must not travel more than one grid cell per time step, or equivalently, the physical characteristic must stay inside the numerical stencil. Necessary for stability but NOT sufficient for all schemes.
  7. Practical evaluation through standardized tests (rotating cone, deformational flow, frontogenesis) that probe different aspects of scheme performance.

Key Takeaways

Takeaway 1

Accuracy ≠ Stability

A scheme can be accurate (small truncation error) but unstable, or inaccurate but stable.

Takeaway 2

Short Waves Are Always Handled Worst

The 2Δx wave has the largest errors in both amplitude and phase.

Takeaway 3

Numerical Diffusion Is the Fatal Flaw

Low-order upwind schemes destroy all features over long integrations.

Takeaway 4

CFL Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

Satisfying CFL doesn't guarantee stability for all schemes (e.g., FTCS is always unstable).

Takeaway 5

Higher-Order Accuracy Pays Off

Halving Δx gives 4× improvement (2nd-order) vs. only 2× (1st-order).

Takeaway 6

Always Test Your Scheme

Use standardized cases before trusting a scheme for real atmospheric simulations.

1. The Big Picture (30-second version)

We simulate one-dimensional heat conduction through three urban substrate columns (asphalt road, concrete roof, bare soil), each 2 metres deep, driven by a synthetic diurnal cycle of solar radiation and air temperature. We solve the heat equation using three different finite-difference schemes (FTCS, BTCS, Crank–Nicolson) at three different time steps (15 s, 60 s, 600 s). The surface temperature is computed at every step by solving the surface energy balance with Newton iteration. We then measure how the schemes' surface ground heat flux deviates from a fine-Δt reference.

Headline finding: at Δt = 600 s, BTCS over-amplifies the diurnal G amplitude by 40 % on asphalt and concrete, 19 % on bare soil; Crank–Nicolson halves these errors. The dominant error is operator-splitting between the column and SEB updates.

2. Sequence of Steps — How the Experiment Runs

The whole project follows this order; everything else is detail.

  1. Define substrates. Three layered stacks of $(\text{depth}, \lambda, C)$.
  2. Build the grid. Per-substrate stretched grid (top cell 0.5 cm for asphalt/concrete, 1 cm for bare soil; geometric stretch 1.18–1.25, bottom cell ≈ 30 cm at $z = 2$ m).
  3. Initialize. Set every cell from the analytical damping-depth profile evaluated at $t = 0$.
  4. Time-step loop. For each step:
    • Sub-step A: advance the column from time $n$ to $n+1$ using the θ-method, holding $T_s^0$ fixed.
    • Sub-step B: solve the surface energy balance for new $T_s^0$ via Newton iteration, holding the column fixed.
    • Apply bottom BC: $T_{N-1} = T_{N-2}$ (zero-flux Neumann).
  5. Integration period. 2 diurnal cycles. Use day-2 for all diagnostics.
  6. Compare. Each scheme's day-2 $G(t)$ and $T_s^0(t)$ against the reference (FTCS at Δt = 15 s).
  7. Diagnose. RMSE of $T_s^0$, peak-amplitude ratio of $G$, daily-storage ratio.

4. The Governing Equation (the physics)

The heat equation in conductivity form (preserves discrete heat conservation across layer interfaces):

$$C_s(z) \, \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left[ \lambda_s(z) \, \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z} \right]$$

In words: the volumetric heat capacity times the rate of temperature change at depth $z$ equals the spatial divergence of the conductive heat flux. Heat piles up wherever more flux enters from above than exits below.

5. The Three Schemes — One Equation, Three α Values

All three time-stepping schemes are special cases of a single θ-method update equation:

$$C_j \Delta z_j \, \frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n}{\Delta t} \;=\; \alpha \, [-\partial G/\partial z]^{n+1} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,[-\partial G/\partial z]^{n}$$

where the flux-divergence is computed cell-by-cell from $G_{j+1/2} = \lambda_{j+1/2}(T_j - T_{j+1})/\Delta z$.

$\nu = \kappa \Delta t / \Delta z^2$ is the dimensionless diffusion number — the diffusion analogue of the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy number.

6. Boundary Conditions — What Closes the System

The heat equation is second-order in $z$, so we need two BCs (one at each end of the column).

6a. Top BC — Surface Energy Balance (SEB) closure

The top of the column is closed by the requirement that the four surface fluxes balance to zero:

$$R_n - H - LE - G = 0 \quad \text{(solved for } T_s^0\text{)}$$

with each flux evaluated at the surface temperature $T_s^0$:

  • Net radiation: $R_n = (1 - \alpha_s)\,S_\downarrow + \varepsilon_s L_\downarrow - \varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^4$. ($\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}$ W/m²/K⁴)
  • Sensible heat: $H = \rho c_p (T_s^0 - T_a)/r_a$ where $r_a = 1/(C_H U) \approx 67$ s/m.
  • Latent heat: $LE = 0$ (strict-impervious assumption — no evaporation).
  • Ground heat: $G = \lambda_{1/2}(T_s^0 - T_1)/(z_1 - z_0)$ — from the top half-level.

The $(T_s^0)^4$ term makes this equation nonlinear. Solved by Newton iteration:

$$T_{s,\,\text{new}}^0 \;=\; T_{s,\,\text{old}}^0 \;-\; F(T_{s,\,\text{old}}^0)/F'(T_{s,\,\text{old}}^0)$$

where $F = R_n - H - LE - G$. Typically converges in 3–4 iterations to $|\Delta T| < 10^{-4}$ K.

6b. Bottom BC — Zero-flux Neumann at $z = 2$ m

At the deepest face, the temperature gradient is set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z}\bigg|_{z=2\,\text{m}} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G\big|_{z=2\,\text{m}} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_{N-1} = T_{N-2}$$

Discretely, one line of code: the deepest cell mirrors the one above it. Justified because $z = 2$ m is 14–25 damping depths down, where the diurnal amplitude is already negligible (~$10^{-6}$ of the surface value).

7. Von Neumann Stability Analysis — How We Used It

We used von Neumann analysis to predict which (scheme, Δt, substrate) combinations would blow up, then verified the prediction empirically.

7a. The procedure (four steps)

  1. Substitute a Fourier mode $T_j^n = A^n \cdot e^{ikj\Delta z}$ into each scheme's update equation.
  2. Cancel common factors and solve for the per-step amplification factor $A$ as a function of $\nu$ and $k\Delta z$.
  3. Demand $|A| \le 1$ for every resolvable wavenumber, i.e., $k\Delta z \in [0, \pi]$.
  4. Worst case is always at $k\Delta z = \pi$ (the 2-Δz wave). Substitute and solve for the stability bound on $\nu$.

9. Algorithm — One Time Step (general form for Test 2)

for step in range(N_steps):
    # SUB-STEP A: column update (theta-method)
    G_half = lambda_half * (T - T_neighbour) / dz_centre     # fluxes at half-levels
    if alpha == 0:
        # FTCS - explicit, no linear system
        T_new = T + dt / (C * dz) * (G_half[:-1] - G_half[1:])
    else:
        # BTCS (alpha=1) or CN (alpha=0.5) - implicit, tridiagonal solve
        A_band, b = build_tridiagonal_system(T, alpha, dt, dz, lambda_half, C)
        T_new = scipy.linalg.solve_banded((1, 1), A_band, b)

    # Apply bottom BC (zero-flux Neumann)
    T_new[N-1] = T_new[N-2]

    # SUB-STEP B: SEB update (Newton iteration for new T_s^0)
    Ts0 = Ts0_old
    for _ in range(MAX_ITER):
        F  = R_n(Ts0) - H(Ts0) - LE - G(Ts0, T_new[1])
        dF = dR_n/dT - dH/dT - dG/dT
        dTs = -F / dF
        Ts0 = Ts0 + dTs
        if abs(dTs) < 1e-4: break

    # Update top cell to the new surface temperature
    T_new[0] = Ts0
    T = T_new
    t = t + dt
    record(Ts0, G_half[0])
Order of operations is the key design choice: column FIRST (holding $T_s^0$ fixed), then SEB SECOND (holding column fixed). This is the operator splitting that produces the first-order error analysed in §5.2 of the report.

10. What the Results Showed (one-page summary)

From Test 1 (verification)

  • FTCS at $\nu$ = 0.4: RMSE_T = 0.007 K — essentially exact.
  • FTCS at $\nu$ = 0.6: BLEW UP at step 53 — exactly as von Neumann predicted.
  • BTCS error grows linearly with Δt (first-order). CN error stays flat (second-order behaviour here).
  • Conclusion: solver passes verification. Trust it on Test 2.

From Test 2 (prognostic SEB)

  • At Δt = 15 s: all three schemes agree to line-thickness on every substrate. Control condition.
  • At Δt = 60 s: FTCS blows up on asphalt and concrete. BTCS and CN run to completion.
  • At Δt = 600 s: FTCS catastrophically unstable on asphalt and concrete; mildly unstable on soil. BTCS over-amplifies the diurnal G amplitude by 40% / 41% / 19% (asphalt / roof / soil). CN halves these errors to 21% / 26% / 10%.
  • Δt-refinement ratios: all six BTCS and CN values cluster in $[8.2, 10.2]$ — first-order in Δt for both schemes, even though CN is intrinsically second-order. Smoking gun for operator-splitting error.
  • SHAP attribution on 150-column synthetic ensemble: $\kappa_{\rm top}$ is the dominant predictor, $R^2 = 0.92$ from $\kappa_{\rm top}$ alone.
  • Daily storage integral preserved within ±5% across all schemes — over-amplification is symmetric, so daily-mean UHI is preserved but diurnal range is biased.

12. Where Does the Initial Temperature at Each Cell Come From?

The initial condition is applied once at $t = 0$. After that, the IC has no further role — the simulation is driven forward by the BCs and the θ-method update.

12a. The formula

We set every cell's temperature using the analytical damping-depth solution evaluated at $t = 0$:

$$T(z, 0) \;=\; T_{\rm mean} \;+\; A_0 \cdot e^{-z/d} \cdot \cos(-z/d)$$

Two pieces multiply $A_0$: the exponential envelope $e^{-z/d}$ which shrinks amplitude with depth, and the cosine $\cos(-z/d)$ which encodes the phase delay with depth.

12b. Worked-out initial values for Test 1

Plugging in $T_{\rm mean} = 292.5$ K, $A_0 = 7.5$ K, $d = 7.97$ cm:

By cell 50 (50 cm down — about 6 damping depths), the initial $T$ is already indistinguishable from the daily mean 292.5 K. By cell 200 (2 m down), it is 292.500000... — flat to machine precision. That is why the column is 2 m deep: 14–25 damping depths is overkill, but it gives the bottom BC nothing physical to do.

12c. Why this IC and not just zero (or $T = T_{\rm mean}$) everywhere?

If we initialised every cell at $T = T_{\rm mean}$ uniformly, the column would not be in equilibrium with the diurnal forcing. The first few simulation days would be spent letting the daily wave penetrate from the surface and the column adjust. That spin-up phase is wasted compute. By starting from the analytical $t = 0$ profile, the column is already in quasi-equilibrium and day-2 (or day-5) diagnostics are clean.

Note: "quasi-equilibrium" — not exact — because our numerical scheme is not the exact analytical solution. There is a tiny mismatch between the IC and what the numerical solver would have produced at $t = 0$ if it had been running forever. That mismatch decays within a few diurnal cycles, which is why we wait until day 5 (Test 1) or day 2 (Test 2) for diagnostics.

13. What is harmonic_mean_lambda?

harmonic_mean_lambda is the effective thermal conductivity at a face between two adjacent cells. It is computed from the two cell-centre conductivities by the harmonic-mean formula:

$$\lambda_{j+1/2} \;=\; \frac{2 \, \lambda_j \, \lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j + \lambda_{j+1}}$$

13a. Why harmonic mean (not arithmetic mean)?

Derivation: consider two materials in series, each obeying Fourier's law. In steady state, the SAME heat flux must flow through both. The temperature drop divides between them in inverse proportion to their conductivities. Solving gives the harmonic-mean formula, which guarantees the discrete flux at the face exactly matches the continuous flux.

Numerical example — concrete deck ($\lambda$ = 1.50) on mineral-wool insulation ($\lambda$ = 0.04):

  • Arithmetic mean: $(1.50 + 0.04)/2 = 0.77$ — over-estimates the flux by a factor of 10.
  • Harmonic mean: $2 \cdot 1.50 \cdot 0.04 / (1.50 + 0.04) = 0.078$ — correctly captures the insulator's choke-point behaviour.
The harmonic mean ensures the discrete code preserves heat conservation across material interfaces. Arithmetic mean would silently destroy energy conservation at every interface between materials with different λ.

13b. What does harmonic_mean_lambda equal for Test 1?

Test 1 uses a single uniform material (sandy loam with $\lambda$ = 0.30 everywhere). At every face $j+1/2$:

$$\lambda_{j+1/2} \;=\; \frac{2 \cdot 0.30 \cdot 0.30}{0.30 + 0.30} \;=\; \frac{0.18}{0.60} \;=\; 0.30 \;\text{W/m/K}$$

So for Test 1, the harmonic mean is trivial — every face has $\lambda$ = 0.30, the same value as every cell centre. The harmonic-mean formula does not do anything interesting here. But we still call the same function in the code, because the SAME solver runs for both Test 1 and Test 2; we do not have one solver for uniform substrates and a different one for layered substrates.

For Test 2 (layered substrates), the harmonic mean matters at every layer interface.

14. Test 1 — The Algorithm at Every Time Step

Below is exactly what runs at every time step in Test 1, in order. Read it as a recipe.

Step 1: Compute the new prescribed surface temperature

Advance time, then plug into the prescribed BC formula:

t_new = t_old + dt
T_s_0_new = T_mean + A_0 * cos(omega * t_new)

This is NOT solved by Newton iteration in Test 1 — it is just plugged in. The surface temperature for Test 1 is externally prescribed, not solved from the SEB.

Step 2: Compute the harmonic-mean λ at every face

for j in range(N - 1):
    lam_half[j] = 2 * lam[j] * lam[j+1] / (lam[j] + lam[j+1])
# For Test 1: every lam_half[j] = 0.30 (uniform).
# For Test 2: lam_half jumps at layer interfaces.

In practice, lam_half is precomputed ONCE at the start (it depends only on the substrate material distribution, not on $T$), so this step does not actually re-run every time.

Step 3: Update interior cells ($j = 1, 2, \ldots, N-2$) using the θ-method

The θ-method update equation:

$$C \cdot \Delta z \cdot \frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^{n}}{\Delta t} \;=\; \alpha \cdot Q_j^{n+1} \;+\; (1-\alpha) \cdot Q_j^{n}$$

where $Q_j$ is the net flux divergence at cell $j$:

$$Q_j \;=\; \frac{\lambda_{j-1/2}(T_{j-1} - T_j)}{\Delta z} \;-\; \frac{\lambda_{j+1/2}(T_j - T_{j+1})}{\Delta z}$$

Two cases:

  • $\alpha = 0$ (FTCS): the right-hand side has only OLD values (time level $n$). We just compute: 
    T_new[j] = T[j] + nu * (T[j-1] - 2*T[j] + T[j+1])
    One pass through the array — cheap.
  • $\alpha = 1$ (BTCS) or $\alpha = 1/2$ (CN): the right-hand side has UNKNOWN $T_{\rm new}$ values too. We must solve a tridiagonal linear system $A \cdot T_{\rm new} = b$. scipy.linalg.solve_banded does this in $O(N)$.

Step 4: Apply the bottom BC at every step

T_new[N-1] = T_new[N-2]   # zero-flux Neumann

Forces the gradient between the deepest two cells to zero → flux at the bottom face is zero. Reapplied at every step (not just once). Without this, the deep cells would drift.

Step 5: Apply the top BC at every step

T_new[0] = T_s_0_new   # prescribed surface value from Step 1

Plug in the value computed in Step 1. Reapplied at every step (the prescribed surface temperature changes sinusoidally throughout the day). Without this, the top cell would freely drift and the simulation would not represent the diurnal forcing.

Step 6: Advance and record

T = T_new
t = t_new
G_surface = lam_half[0] * (T[0] - T[1]) / dz   # surface ground heat flux
record(t, T[0], G_surface, ...)
if t < t_end: goto Step 1

16. Test 1 — Concrete Numbers for One Δt = 60 s Step

To make this absolutely concrete, here is what one Δt = 60 s step on Test 1 actually computes, starting from the analytical IC at $t = 0$:

Before the first step ($t = 0$)

  • $T[0] = 300.00$ K (set by the IC: $T_{\rm mean} + A_0 \cdot e^{0} \cdot \cos 0$)
  • $T[1] = 299.07$ K, $T[5] = 295.75$ K, $T[10] = 293.16$ K, $T[200] = 292.50$ K (also from IC)
  • All cells have $\lambda = 0.30$, so all $\lambda_{\rm half} = 0.30$ (computed once at start)

Step 1 — Compute new $T_s^0$

$t_{\rm new} = 60$ s. $\omega t_{\rm new} = 7.27\!\times\!10^{-5} \times 60 = 0.00436$ rad. $\cos(0.00436) \approx 0.99999$.

So $T_{s,\,\rm new}^0 = 292.5 + 7.5 \times 0.99999 \approx 299.9999$ K. The surface temperature barely changes in 60 s (still near its peak).

Step 2 — Harmonic-mean λ

Already done at start: all $\lambda_{\rm half} = 0.30$. No work this step.

Step 3 — Update interior cells (FTCS, $\alpha = 0$)

For Test 1 at $\nu = 0.139$ (i.e., $\Delta t = 60$ s, well below the bound): per cell,

$$T_{\rm new}[j] \;=\; T[j] \;+\; \nu \cdot (T[j-1] - 2\,T[j] + T[j+1])$$

Worked example for cell $j = 5$:

$T_{\rm new}[5] = 295.75 + 0.139 \cdot (296.50 - 2\cdot 295.75 + 294.78) = 295.75 + 0.139 \cdot (-0.22) = 295.72$ K

Cell 5 cools slightly (~0.03 K in 60 s) — consistent with the daily wave propagating downward.

Step 4 — Apply bottom BC

$T_{\rm new}[200] = T_{\rm new}[199]$ ($\approx 292.50$ K both — bottom barely moves)

Step 5 — Apply top BC

$T_{\rm new}[0] = T_{s,\,\rm new}^0 = 299.9999$ K. Replaces whatever the interior update computed for cell 0.

Step 6 — Advance

$T \leftarrow T_{\rm new}$. $t \leftarrow 60$ s. Record $T[0] = 299.9999$ K, $G_{\rm surface} = 0.30 \cdot (299.9999 - T_{\rm new}[1])/0.01$ W/m². Loop back to Step 1.

17. Why This Matters for Verification

After integrating for 5 days following the recipe above, we compare the day-5 cycle of $T(z=10 \text{ cm}, t)$ and $G(z=0, t)$ against the analytical damping-depth solution. The match should be excellent if:

  • The IC was in quasi-equilibrium → no spin-up artefact remains by day 5.
  • The harmonic-mean and flux divergence are correctly coded → no energy-conservation errors.
  • The top BC is re-applied every step → the surface temperature tracks the prescribed sinusoid.
  • The bottom BC zeroes the deep flux → no spurious leakage out the bottom.
  • The chosen $\nu$ (for FTCS) is below 1/2 → no exponential noise growth.
If any of these is broken, Test 1 fails. The whole point of Test 1 is to catch implementation bugs before we move on to Test 2 (whose results have no closed-form to check against).

20. The Surface Energy Balance Closure — How $T_s^0$ Gets Solved Each Step

This is the biggest conceptual difference between Test 1 and Test 2. In Test 1 we PRESCRIBED $T_s^0$; in Test 2 we SOLVE for it at every step.

20a. The equation we are solving

At every time step, $T_s^0$ must satisfy the surface energy balance:

$$F(T_s^0) \;\equiv\; R_n(T_s^0) - H(T_s^0) - LE - G(T_s^0) \;=\; 0$$

Each term depends on $T_s^0$:

  • $R_n = (1 - \alpha_s) S_\downarrow + \varepsilon_s L_\downarrow - \varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^4$   (the $T_s^4$ makes this nonlinear)
  • $H = \rho c_p (T_s^0 - T_a) / r_a$   (linear in $T_s^0$)
  • $LE = 0$   (strict-impervious assumption)
  • $G = \lambda_{1/2} (T_s^0 - T_1) / (z_1 - z_0)$   (linear in $T_s^0$ — $T_1$ is the cell-1 temperature held fixed during the SEB iteration)

20b. Why we cannot just "plug in a value" like in Test 1

In Test 1, we set $T_s^0(t) = 292.5 + 7.5\cos(\omega t)$ — direct evaluation, one line of code. In Test 2, the surface temperature is the unknown: we have an equation, not a formula. The four flux terms must balance, and only one $T_s^0$ value makes them balance. We must solve the equation.

20c. Newton iteration — how it solves the equation

Newton's method is a textbook root-finder. Starting from a guess $T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0$, we compute a correction by linearising $F$ around the guess:

$$T_{s,\,{\rm new}}^0 \;=\; T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0 \;-\; \frac{F(T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0)}{F'(T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0)}$$

The derivative (Jacobian) is computed analytically — no finite differences needed:

$$F'(T_s^0) \;=\; -4 \varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^3 \;-\; \frac{\rho c_p}{r_a} \;-\; \frac{\lambda_{1/2}}{z_1 - z_0}$$

(All three terms are negative — sign comes from how each flux responds to a warmer surface: hotter $T_s^0$ → more outgoing radiation, more sensible heating loss to the air, more ground flux into the substrate.)

20d. A walked-through iteration example

Example for asphalt road at $t = 18:00$ LT (just after sunset). Atmospheric forcing at that time: $S_\downarrow = 0$ (sun is down), $L_\downarrow ≈ 340$ W/m², $T_a ≈ 25$ °C = 298.15 K. The top cell temperature $T_1$ (held fixed during the SEB iteration) is some warm value, say 35 °C = 308.15 K. Initial guess for $T_s^0$: the value from the previous step, say 35.5 °C = 308.65 K.

Iteration 1: Compute $F$ at $T_s^0 = 308.65$ K.

  • $R_n = 0.95 \cdot 340 - 0.95 \cdot 5.67\!\times\!10^{-8} \cdot 308.65^4 = 323 - 488 = -165$ W/m²
  • $H = 1.2 \cdot 1005 \cdot (308.65 - 298.15) / 67 = 188$ W/m²
  • $G = 0.75 \cdot (308.65 - 308.15) / 0.005 = 75$ W/m²
  • $F = R_n - H - 0 - G = -165 - 188 - 75 = -428$ W/m² (way out of balance — surface losing energy faster than it can replace)
  • $F' = -4 \cdot 0.95 \cdot 5.67\!\times\!10^{-8} \cdot 308.65^3 - 1.2\!\cdot\!1005/67 - 0.75/0.005 ≈ -6.32 - 18 - 150 = -174.3$ W/m²/K
  • $\Delta T = -F/F' = -(-428)/(-174.3) = -2.46$ K
  • New guess: $T_s^0 = 308.65 - 2.46 = 306.19$ K (the surface drops 2.46 K toward balance)

Iteration 2: Compute $F$ at $T_s^0 = 306.19$ K. The new $F$ value is much smaller, ~ 10–20 W/m². $\Delta T \approx 0.1$ K.

Iteration 3: $F$ ≈ 0.1 W/m², $\Delta T$ ≈ $10^{-3}$ K.

Iteration 4: $|\Delta T| < 10^{-4}$ K → STOP. Newton converged to the new surface temperature, e.g., $T_s^0 \approx 306.08$ K. This becomes the value used in the next time step's sub-step A.

Newton's method converges quadratically: the error roughly squares each iteration. In practice on the SEB, 3–4 iterations are enough. The tolerance $|\Delta T| < 10^{-4}$ K is far tighter than any other error in the simulation.

22. Test 2 — The Algorithm at Every Time Step (Two Sub-Steps)

Each Δt step is split into two sub-steps executed in this order:

Sub-step A — column update (holding $T_s^0$ FIXED at its OLD value)

t_new = t_old + dt
T_s_0_old = T[0]                  # surface T from end of previous step

# Apply top BC: cell 0 gets T_s_0_old (held fixed for the column update)
T[0] = T_s_0_old

# Build the right-hand side using harmonic-mean fluxes
for j in range(N - 1):
    G_half[j] = lambda_half[j] * (T[j] - T[j+1]) / dz_centre[j]

# Apply theta-method
if alpha == 0:
    # FTCS - explicit
    T_new = T + dt / (C * dz) * (G_half[:-1] - G_half[1:])
else:
    # BTCS or CN - implicit, tridiagonal solve
    A_band, b = build_tridiagonal_system(T, alpha, dt, lambda_half, C, dz)
    T_new = scipy.linalg.solve_banded((1, 1), A_band, b)

# Apply bottom BC: zero-flux Neumann
T_new[N-1] = T_new[N-2]

What sub-step A does: advances every interior cell from time $n$ to $n+1$ using the θ-method, assuming the surface temperature is still at its old value. After sub-step A, cells 1..N-1 have their new values, but cell 0 still has $T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0$.

Sub-step B — SEB Newton iteration (holding the COLUMN FIXED at its NEW values)

T_s = T_new[0]                   # initial guess: old surface T
for k in range(MAX_ITER):
    # Compute atmospheric forcing at the NEW time
    Sd = S_down(t_new)
    Ld = L_down(t_new)
    Ta = T_air(t_new)
    U  = U_wind(t_new)
    r_a = 1 / (C_H * U)

    # Compute residual F(T_s)
    R_n = (1 - alpha_s) * Sd + emis * Ld - emis * sigma * T_s**4
    H   = rho * c_p * (T_s - Ta) / r_a
    G   = lambda_half[0] * (T_s - T_new[1]) / dz_centre[0]
    F   = R_n - H - 0 - G

    # Compute analytical Jacobian F'(T_s)
    dF = -4 * emis * sigma * T_s**3 - rho * c_p / r_a - lambda_half[0] / dz_centre[0]

    # Newton update
    dT = -F / dF
    T_s = T_s + dT

    if abs(dT) < 1e-4:
        break    # converged

# After convergence, T_s holds the NEW surface temperature
T_new[0] = T_s

What sub-step B does: solves the SEB for the new surface temperature, with the column held fixed at the values just computed in sub-step A. After sub-step B, cell 0 has its new $T_s^0$ consistent with the just-updated column.

End of step

T = T_new
t = t_new
record(t, T[0], G_half[0])
if t < t_end: goto Sub-step A
The order matters. The column is advanced first, THEN the SEB updates the surface. This sequence is the "operator splitting" between the column-update operator A and the SEB-update operator B. The two operators do not commute, and approximating $e^{(A+B)\Delta t}$ as $e^{A\Delta t}\,e^{B\Delta t}$ introduces a first-order error in $\Delta t$ — regardless of how accurate each individual sub-step is. This is the dominant error at large $\Delta t$ in this project.

24. Test 2 — One Concrete Δt = 600 s Step (BTCS on Asphalt Road)

To make Test 2 absolutely concrete, here is one BTCS step on the asphalt road at Δt = 600 s, starting from a quasi-equilibrium state just after sunset.

Before the step ($t = 64\,800$ s = 18:00 LT on day 2)

  • $T[0] = T_{s,\,{\rm old}}^0 = 308.65$ K (35.5 °C — asphalt still warm from afternoon sun)
  • $T[1] \approx 308.15$ K (top half of the 5 cm asphalt layer, cooled slightly)
  • $T[6] \approx 305.0$ K (in the aggregate layer)
  • $T[13] \approx 297.0$ K (in dry soil)
  • $T[N-1] \approx 292.5$ K (deep, essentially at daily mean)
  • $\lambda_{\rm half}$ precomputed; sub-surface jumps at faces 5/6 (asphalt/aggregate) and 12/13 (aggregate/soil).

Sub-step A — column update, BTCS, $\Delta t = 600$ s

  1. Hold $T[0]$ fixed at 308.65 K.
  2. Build the tridiagonal matrix $A$ and right-hand side $b$ from the BTCS formula. The diagonal entries depend on $C_j$, $\Delta z_j$, $\Delta t$, and harmonic-mean $\lambda$. Cells across layer interfaces (j=5/6 and j=12/13) get the harmonic-mean values 0.977 and 0.494 in their off-diagonals — exactly the values from §21a.
  3. Call scipy.linalg.solve_banded((1,1), A, b) to solve for $T_{\rm new}[0\ldots N-1]$. $O(N)$ cost — about 0.1 ms for $N$ = 28 cells.
  4. Apply bottom BC: $T_{\rm new}[N-1] = T_{\rm new}[N-2]$. (Both ≈ 292.5 K — no change.)
  5. Cell 1 cools, say to $T_{\rm new}[1] \approx 305.2$ K (a 2.95 K drop in 600 s — heat being conducted downward).

At the end of sub-step A, $T_{\rm new}[0]$ is still 308.65 K (held fixed by the BC during this sub-step).

Sub-step B — SEB Newton iteration

Now we update $T[0]$ to its proper new value. Compute atmospheric forcing at $t_{\rm new} = 65\,400$ s (= 18:10 LT). The sun is fully below the horizon: $S_\downarrow = 0$. $T_a$ has dropped to about 297.5 K (24.4 °C). $L_\downarrow \approx 338$ W/m².

Initial guess: $T_s^0 = T_{\rm new}[0] = 308.65$ K (the value from sub-step A — surface still at the old afternoon temperature).

Iteration 1:

  • $R_n = 0 + 0.95 \cdot 338 - 0.95 \cdot 5.67\!\times\!10^{-8} \cdot 308.65^4 \approx 321 - 488 = -167$ W/m²
  • $H = 1.2 \cdot 1005 \cdot (308.65 - 297.5) / 67 \approx 200$ W/m²
  • $G = 0.75 \cdot (308.65 - 305.2) / 0.005 = 517$ W/m² (large because the column just cooled below the surface, creating a steep gradient!)
  • $F = -167 - 200 - 0 - 517 = -884$ W/m² — surface is losing energy at 884 W/m²; needs to drop fast.
  • $F' \approx -174$ W/m²/K (same form as §20d).
  • $\Delta T = -F/F' = -(-884)/(-174) = -5.08$ K.
  • New guess: $T_s^0 = 308.65 - 5.08 = 303.57$ K.

Iteration 2: Compute $F$ at 303.57 K. The drop has reduced all three loss terms; $F \approx -30$ W/m². $\Delta T \approx -0.17$ K. New guess: 303.40 K.

Iteration 3: $F \approx 0.3$ W/m², $\Delta T \approx 2\!\times\!10^{-3}$ K. New guess: 303.398 K.

Iteration 4: $|\Delta T| < 10^{-4}$ K. CONVERGED. $T_s^0 = 303.398$ K = 30.25 °C.

End of step

  • Set $T_{\rm new}[0] = 303.398$ K.
  • Final column state: $T[0] = 303.398$, $T[1] = 305.2$, ... (note the inversion! The cell ABOVE is colder than the cell BELOW because sub-step A let the column "think" $T_s^0$ was still 308.65 K, then sub-step B revealed the surface had actually dropped to 303.4 K. The cell-1 value of 305.2 K is therefore too warm — this is exactly the operator-splitting error.)
  • Record: $t = 65\,400$ s, $T_s^0 = 303.4$ K, $G_{\rm surface} = 0.75 \cdot (303.4 - 305.2)/0.005 = -270$ W/m² (negative — heat flowing OUT of the substrate, since surface is now cooler than the deck).
  • Advance: $t \leftarrow 65\,400$ s, $T \leftarrow T_{\rm new}$. Loop.
The over-amplification on display. A true coupled solver (no splitting) would have advanced the column AND surface together, ending with $T[0] \approx 303.4$ and $T[1]$ closer to 306 K (smaller gradient). Our split solver ends with $T[0] = 303.4$ but $T[1] = 305.2$ — a steeper gradient at the top face. So $G$ is over-amplified at sunset. Over a full day this asymmetric over-amplification produces the 40% diurnal-amplitude inflation in $G$ that the report's Figure 4 shows on asphalt and concrete.

25. Why This Matters — The Operator-Splitting Error

The whole point of Test 2 is to measure how the schemes compare at operational Δt. The headline finding is:

  • At Δt = 15 s (control): all three schemes agree to line-thickness. Confirms the implementation is consistent.
  • At Δt = 60 s: FTCS blows up on asphalt ($\nu = 0.9$) and concrete ($\nu = 1.71$); soil ($\nu = 0.14$) survives. BTCS and CN run to completion with small errors.
  • At Δt = 600 s: FTCS catastrophically unstable on asphalt and concrete, mildly unstable on soil. BTCS over-amplifies the diurnal $G$ amplitude by 40 % (asphalt), 41 % (roof), 19 % (soil). CN halves these to 21 %, 26 %, 10 %.

Why both BTCS and CN show first-order error in Δt

BTCS is intrinsically first-order — that's expected. But CN is intrinsically second-order, so its error should shrink as $\Delta t^2$. Empirically it shrinks as $\Delta t^1$. Why?

Because the operator-splitting error is first-order, and it dominates over CN's per-substep error. Both BTCS and CN show $\Delta t$-refinement ratios of about 10 (not 100) when $\Delta t$ scales by 10×. The dominant error is the splitting between column and SEB, not the per-substep truncation. CN's second-order accuracy is wasted.

How to fix it (sketched but not implemented in this project)

  • Option A — fully coupled solver: augment the tridiagonal system with one extra row coupling $T_s^0$ to $T_1$ via the SEB. One linear solve per step instead of "column then Newton". Eliminates the splitting error.
  • Option B — Strang symmetric splitting: half-step column (Δt/2) → full-step SEB (Δt) → half-step column (Δt/2). The symmetric form cancels the leading commutator error, making the splitting second-order. Same per-step cost as the asymmetric form.

SHAP attribution — confirming the mechanism with data

To confirm that $\kappa_{\rm top}$ is the dominant substrate property driving the error, the project ran a separate analysis: 150 random three-layer substrate columns, BTCS at Δt = 600 s. Gradient-boosted regression with SHAP attribution found that $\kappa_{\rm top}$ alone explains $R^2 = 0.92$ of the variance in BTCS coarse-Δt error. This is consistent with the operator-splitting picture: high $\kappa_{\rm top}$ means the top cell relaxes fast to the (held-fixed) $T_s^0$ during sub-step A, then has to "catch up" to the (now-correct) $T_s^0$ at sub-step B — producing a large gradient swing and a large $G$ error. The mechanistic story and the data-driven story converge on the same answer.

The Test 2 deep dive is the project's mechanistic core. Once you understand the two-sub-step structure (§22), the SEB Newton iteration (§20), and the operator-splitting error (§25), every plot and table in §4-§6 of the long report falls into place.

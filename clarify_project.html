Introduction

This guide walks through the numerical modeling of one-dimensional heat conduction in urban substrates — pavement, concrete roofs, soil — coupled to a surface energy balance. It is written for anyone learning the subject for the first time. Every concept, every equation, every notation is explained from scratch.

For every major equation, this document tells you (a) the status of the equation — is it an established theoretical formula, derived in this document, or a definition? — and (b) what every symbol means and what each line of math is saying in plain English.

Part 1. Foundational physics: heat, temperature, and the substrate

Why this part exists — and what you will get from it

Before we can write a single line of numerical code, we have to agree on what the code is trying to compute. Part 1 builds that vocabulary from scratch. It answers four basic questions: (1) what is heat conduction in plain language, (2) what physical numbers describe a substrate (asphalt, concrete, soil), (3) how far does the daily heating cycle reach into the ground, and (4) how does the surface decide its own temperature.

Why we need this before anything else. Every choice further down — the size of grid cells, the size of time steps, the choice of scheme, the boundary conditions — is dictated by the physical scales established here. If you skip the physics, the numerics look arbitrary; if you understand the physics, the numerics look inevitable.

How it connects to the project. The damping depth $d \approx 8$–$14$ cm computed in Section 1.3 forces us to pick a sub-centimetre grid; the resulting cell size will combine with $\kappa$ in Section 1.4 to produce the famous 17-second FTCS bound; the SEB in Section 1.5 will turn out to be the place where the operator-splitting error lives. So Part 1 is the source of every constraint the rest of the project responds to.

1.1 What does it mean for the ground to conduct heat?

What we are doing in this subsection. Before we can write equations for heat flow, we need a physical picture. The next two paragraphs give you that picture in plain language — a hot paving stone, a temperature gradient, and the rate at which heat moves down through the material. By the end of the subsection you will know what the symbol $G$ means and why it is the headline quantity in the entire project.

Imagine a paving stone in the sun. The top is hot — say $50 ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Five centimetres down, the stone is much cooler — say $30 ^\circ\mathrm{C}$. There is a temperature gradient inside the stone. As long as that gradient exists, heat will flow from the hot top to the cool bottom. This flow is conduction.

The amount of heat that flows per square metre per second is the conductive heat flux, denoted $G$ in this project. The symbol $G$ is just a label — a single letter that stands for "ground heat flux at this location, at this time". Its units are watts per square metre, $\mathrm{W/m^{2}}$. By convention $G > 0$ means flow downward (into the ground).

To make this less abstract: a typical mid-afternoon $G$ on a sun-warmed asphalt road is about $200$ W/m² (positive, into the ground). At night the same surface emits at perhaps $-50$ W/m² (negative, out of the ground).

Wrapping up Section 1.1. You now have one number — $G$, the ground heat flux — and one intuition — heat flows from hot to cold whenever there is a temperature gradient. Everything in the rest of the project is built around computing $G$ accurately. The next step is to name the material properties that decide how big $G$ can be for a given gradient.

1.2 The four key material properties

What we are doing in this subsection. $G$ depends on the gradient, but it also depends on what the substrate is made of. A 1 °C/cm gradient through wet concrete moves much more heat than the same gradient through dry styrofoam. We need four material numbers — three actual material properties ($\lambda$, $C$, $\kappa$) and one derived length scale ($d$) — to capture that dependence. You will use these four numbers everywhere downstream: $\lambda$ in Fourier's law, $C$ in the heat-storage term, $\kappa$ in the stability bound, $d$ in choosing how fine the top grid cell needs to be.

Every substrate is described by two material numbers, from which a third is derived. The fourth quantity is a length scale.

Thermal conductivity $\lambda$ (Greek letter "lambda"). Units W/m/K. The number $\lambda$ tells you how much heat flows through the material per unit temperature gradient. Bigger $\lambda$ means heat propagates more easily. Air $\approx 0.025$; rigid foam $\approx 0.03$; dry sandy soil $\approx 0.30$; asphalt $\approx 0.75$; concrete $\approx 1.5$; granite $\approx 3.0$.

Volumetric heat capacity $C$. Units J/m³/K. The number $C$ tells you how much energy (in joules) is needed to raise one cubic metre of the material by one Kelvin. Water $\approx 4.18 \times 10^{6}$; concrete $\approx 2.1 \times 10^{6}$; dry sandy soil $\approx 1.3 \times 10^{6}$.

Thermal diffusivity $\kappa$ (Greek letter "kappa"). Defined as

$$\kappa = \frac{\lambda}{C}.$$

Status: [Definition]. $\kappa$ is just a name for the ratio $\lambda/C$.

Reading this equation. Left side: the symbol $\kappa$, a new quantity we are giving a name to. Right side: the ratio $\lambda/C$ where $\lambda$ has units W/m/K = (J/s)/m/K and $C$ has units J/m³/K. The ratio's units work out to m²/s. The physical meaning: if a substrate has high $\lambda$ but also large $C$ (a big reservoir to heat up), the temperature changes only slowly even though heat flows quickly. The ratio $\kappa = \lambda/C$ is the single number that controls how fast the temperature itself responds.

For our project's substrates: $\kappa$ is in the range $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-6}$ m²/s.

Diurnal damping depth $d$. The depth at which the daily temperature wave decays to about 37% of its surface amplitude.

$$d = \sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega}}, \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{86400\ \text{s}} \approx 7.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{rad/s}.$$

Status: [Established theoretical formula], derived from solving the heat equation in a semi-infinite medium with sinusoidal Dirichlet forcing (Carslaw & Jaeger 1959 Section 2.6; Hillel 2003). Re-derived in Part 8.1.

Reading this equation. Left side: $d$, a length (metres). Right side: $\sqrt{ }$ is the square-root symbol; $2$ is the constant 2; $\kappa$ is thermal diffusivity; $\omega$ (Greek "omega") is the angular frequency of the daily cycle. Because one full cycle is $2\pi$ radians and one day is $86 400$ seconds, $\omega = 2\pi / 86 400 \approx 7.27 \times 10^{-5}$ radians per second. The square-root structure says: faster-diffusing materials (larger $\kappa$) feel the surface signal deeper; faster cycles (larger $\omega$) decay closer to the surface.

What does "37% of surface amplitude" actually mean? — the plain-language version

Step 1 — What "amplitude" means in the first place. Temperature at any one place changes through the day: warm in the afternoon, cool before dawn. Plot it over 24 hours and it looks like a wavy line — closest to a cosine curve. The amplitude of that curve is half of the total peak-to-trough swing. Why half? Because the curve goes equally above and below its daily mean. If the mean is 25 °C and the amplitude is 7 K, then peaks are at $25 + 7 = 32$ °C and troughs are at $25 - 7 = 18$ °C — a 14 K peak-to-trough swing, of which the amplitude is 7 K (one side of the swing).

Step 2 — "Surface amplitude" means the amplitude right at the top of the substrate. The top of the asphalt, the top of the concrete deck, the top of the bare soil. That is the value of "amplitude" at $z = 0$ in our formula — the symbol $A$ in the analytical solution. It is the largest amplitude anywhere in the column: the surface gets the full daily forcing without any damping yet.

Step 3 — "37% of surface amplitude" means the swing at depth $d$ is 37% as wide as the swing at the top. The substrate's daily mean temperature is essentially the same at every depth (it has no choice — energy averaged over a full day must balance), but the swing around that mean shrinks as you go down. At $z = d$, the swing is exactly $0.368 \times$ (the swing at $z = 0$).

A concrete worked example — bare soil at our project's site. Bare soil has $d = 7.97$ cm (computed in §1.3). Imagine the daily surface temperature swings from 18 °C at dawn to 32 °C at mid-afternoon. Then:

Reading the table in plain words.

  • At the surface (top row): a hot summer day's worth of swing — 14 K from dawn to mid-afternoon. That is what your hand feels on the asphalt.
  • At 8 cm depth ($\approx d$ for soil): the swing has shrunk to about 5 K. You can still feel it as cooler in the morning, warmer in the afternoon, but the contrast is much weaker.
  • At 16 cm depth ($\approx 2d$): only about 2 K of swing — barely noticeable.
  • By 32 cm depth ($\approx 4d$): the daily swing is under 0.3 K. The temperature is effectively the daily mean, all the time.
  • At 80 cm depth ($\approx 10d$): the daily wave is gone. The temperature follows the seasonal cycle, not the daily one. This is also why root cellars stay cool in summer and don't freeze in winter — they are below the diurnal damping zone.

An intuitive metaphor — shouting through walls. Imagine shouting at the surface of the ground. The wall of soil between you and a listener at depth $z$ muffles your voice. Each additional damping-depth of soil reduces your voice's loudness by a factor of $e^{-1} \approx 0.37$. After one damping-depth, your voice is at 37% of its original loudness. After two, $0.37 \times 0.37 = 13.5\%$. After three, $0.37^{3} = 5\%$. The ground is doing the same thing to the sun's daily forcing: each $d$ of depth attenuates the daily wave by a factor of $1/e$.

An intuitive metaphor — ripples on a pond. Drop a pebble at the centre of a pond. The ripples have a clear amplitude at the centre and weaken as they travel outward. If you measured the wave height every metre, you would find the ripples have lost a fixed fraction of their height per metre travelled. The diurnal wave in the ground is doing the same thing in depth — losing the same fraction per damping-depth $d$ travelled. The difference is that in the ground, $d$ depends on the material: faster-diffusing materials (high $\kappa$) let the wave travel deeper before fading.

How this connects to the rest of the project — the short version. Each substrate's damping depth fixes the size of the top grid cell. That cell size, in turn, fixes the largest stable FTCS time step. The very small $d$ values (8–14 cm) force very small cells (0.5–1 cm), which force unusably small explicit time steps (17 seconds). This is why the project must use implicit schemes. The detailed walkthrough is in the next box.

The full chain, step by step — how "37% at depth $d$" forces every numerical choice in the project

The summary paragraph above said "the 37% rule is the upstream reason for every numerical struggle in the report". That probably sounded like a leap. Below is the complete chain of reasoning, broken into five steps, with concrete numbers at each step.

Step 1 — Each substrate has its own damping depth. The formula $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ gives a different number for each material, because each material has a different $\kappa$. For this project we computed:

SubstrateTop-layer $\kappa$ (m²/s)Damping depth $d$
Bare sandy soil$2.31 \times 10^{-7}$7.97 cm
Asphalt road (top layer)$3.75 \times 10^{-7}$10.16 cm
Concrete roof (top deck)$7.14 \times 10^{-7}$14.02 cm

So the concrete roof has the deepest reach (14 cm) because the concrete deck's $\kappa$ is the largest — heat diffuses fastest through concrete, so the daily wave is felt deeper. Bare soil has the shallowest reach (8 cm) because $\kappa$ is smallest — soil holds the wave near the surface. Asphalt sits in between at 10 cm.

Step 2 — Why grid cells must be much thinner than $d$ — the "averaging" argument, explained.

To simulate the substrate on a computer, we chop the depth axis into cells. Each cell is one row of the computer's array, and it stores one number — the temperature at the centre of that cell. The cell is treated as if it had that single temperature uniformly throughout its thickness.

Imagine choosing 5 cm cells in the bare-soil column. The top cell would run from $z = 0$ (the surface) down to $z = 5$ cm. Within that single cell, the amplitude factor $e^{-z/d}$ changes a lot:

  • At $z = 0$ (top of cell): amplitude factor = $e^{0} = 1.000$ — full 100% of surface amplitude.
  • At $z = 2.5$ cm (centre of cell): amplitude factor = $e^{-2.5/7.97} = 0.731$ — 73% of surface amplitude.
  • At $z = 5$ cm (bottom of cell): amplitude factor = $e^{-5/7.97} = 0.535$ — only 54% of surface amplitude.

The cell forces a single amplitude (the value at the cell centre, 73%) to stand in for a real physical wave that varies from 100% to 54% across the same 5 cm. That is the "averaging" the summary paragraph mentioned — the cell averages out the variation it cannot resolve.

Why this matters numerically. The thing the project most cares about — the ground heat flux $G$ — is the temperature gradient at the surface, $G = -\lambda \, \partial T/\partial z |_{z=0}$. The discrete version of that gradient is $(T_{\rm surface} - T_{\rm first\ cell})/\Delta z$. If the first cell is 5 cm thick and stores a single temperature that already misses 27 percentage points of amplitude (54% gap from 100%), the discrete gradient is wildly wrong. The very signal the project is trying to measure (the diurnal $G$ cycle) is destroyed before any time-stepping scheme is even chosen.

The fix. Make the top cell much thinner than $d$. The project does this with a stretched (geometric) grid: the top cell is 0.5–1 cm, and each cell below it is 1.18–1.25× thicker than the one above. The cell sizes the project actually uses in run_experiments.py are:

SubstrateTop-cell $\Delta z_0$Stretch ratioBottom cell at $z = 2$ m$\Delta z_0 / d$Amplitude variation across the top cell
Bare soil1.0 cm1.25≈ 30 cm12.6%100% → 88.2% (12 pp drop)

The top cell is at most about 12% of a damping depth, so the amplitude varies by at most 12 percentage points across that cell — manageable averaging error. Recall that 5 cm soil cells would have been a 47-point drop, which would have destroyed the discrete gradient.

Why stretched, not uniform. A uniform 0.5 cm grid would need 400 cells to reach $z = 2$ m. The stretched grid uses only 24–30 cells while keeping a fine top and a coarse bottom — where the signal is small and a coarse cell is fine. This is standard practice in land-surface schemes (Best et al. 2011; Masson 2000).

Note: Test 1 (verification) is different. The verification test in §4.1 of the report uses a uniform 1 cm grid on a uniform sandy-loam column. That choice was made so the comparison with the analytical damping-depth solution is clean — no stretching, no layer jumps, just the time-stepping schemes being compared.

Rule of thumb. The top grid cell must be at least ~8–10× thinner than the damping depth. For our three substrates (8–14 cm), this rule produces top cells of 0.5–1 cm, exactly the project's choice.

Step 3 — The FTCS stability bound, and where it comes from.

Now we know $\Delta z_{\rm top} = 0.5$ cm. The next question is: given that cell size, how large a time step $\Delta t$ can the FTCS scheme take without blowing up? The answer is fixed by a formula:

$$\boxed{\;\Delta t_{\max} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta z^{2}}{\kappa}\;}$$

Status of this formula. It is derived — but the derivation uses a theoretically established technique called von Neumann stability analysis (John von Neumann, 1947). The technique itself is the textbook-standard method for analysing finite-difference schemes; every numerical-PDE course teaches it, and Section 6.5 of this walkthrough does the derivation from scratch. The output of that derivation is the inequality

$$\nu \;=\; \frac{\kappa\,\Delta t}{\Delta z^{2}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2}.$$

Where the $1/2$ on the right side comes from. Von Neumann analysis substitutes a Fourier mode $A^{n} e^{ikj\Delta z}$ into the FTCS update equation and demands $|A| \le 1$ for every wavelength the grid can resolve. The worst-case wavelength is the shortest one — exactly $2\Delta z$, the wave that oscillates between adjacent cells. At that wavelength the amplification factor is $A_{\rm FTCS} = 1 - 4\nu$. Demanding $|1 - 4\nu| \le 1$ gives $0 \le \nu \le 1/2$. So the $1/2$ is not arbitrary — it is forced by the algebra of the worst-case Fourier mode in a centred-space, forward-time discretisation of the diffusion equation. The full derivation is in Section 6.5 of this walkthrough.

Summary. The formula $\Delta t_{\max} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta z^{2}/\kappa$ is a derived result from a theoretically established analysis technique. It is not empirical, not fitted to data, not a rule of thumb. It is an algebraic consequence of the FTCS update equation and the diffusion PDE.

Step 4 — Plugging in the numbers for the concrete deck.

With $\Delta z = 0.5$ cm $= 0.005$ m and $\kappa_{\rm concrete} = 7.14 \times 10^{-7}$ m²/s:

$$\Delta t_{\max} \;=\; \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{(0.005)^{2}}{7.14 \times 10^{-7}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2.5 \times 10^{-5}}{7.14 \times 10^{-7}} \;=\; \frac{1}{2} \cdot 35.0 \;\approx\; 17.5 \text{ s}.$$

That is the "17 seconds" the summary mentions. It is the largest time step FTCS can take on a 0.5 cm concrete-deck cell without the simulation blowing up. Take a step of 18 s and the worst-case $2\Delta z$ wave grows by factor $|1 - 4 \cdot (1.029)| = 3.11$ per step — within 30 steps the simulation overflows.

The same calculation for the other substrates' top cells:

  • Bare soil, $\Delta z = 1.0$ cm, $\kappa = 2.31 \times 10^{-7}$: $\Delta t_{\max} = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^{2}/2.31\!\times\!10^{-7} \approx 216$ s.
  • Asphalt road top, $\Delta z = 0.5$ cm, $\kappa = 3.75 \times 10^{-7}$: $\Delta t_{\max} = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.005)^{2}/3.75\!\times\!10^{-7} \approx 33$ s.
  • Concrete deck top, $\Delta z = 0.5$ cm, $\kappa = 7.14 \times 10^{-7}$: $\Delta t_{\max} \approx 17$ s — the tightest of the three.

Step 5 — The whole chain in one line.

$$\underbrace{\text{small } d}_{\text{physics}} \;\Rightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{small } \Delta z}_{\text{grid choice}} \;\Rightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{small } \Delta t_{\max}}_{\text{FTCS stability}} \;\Rightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{FTCS unusable}}_{\text{conclusion}}.$$

Operational mesoscale models use $\Delta t = 60$ s. Regional climate models use $\Delta t = 600$ s. Both exceed the FTCS bound on every substrate's top cell. So FTCS is structurally ruled out at operational time steps — not because of a "tuning issue", but because the physics ($d$ is 8–14 cm) forces a chain of constraints that ends at "FTCS cannot keep up". This is the same chain that motivates the whole project: it is why we must compare BTCS and CN, both of which are unconditionally stable and therefore not bound by $\Delta t \le 17$ s.

So when the previous paragraph said "the 37% rule is the upstream physical reason for every numerical struggle in the rest of the report", this is what it meant. The 37% defines $d$; $d$ defines $\Delta z$; $\Delta z$ defines $\Delta t_{\max}$ for FTCS; $\Delta t_{\max}$ for FTCS is too small for operational use; therefore we must study BTCS and CN — which is the project.

Why stretched, not uniform. Two competing requirements:

  • Top of the column must resolve the 8–14 cm damping depth → top cell must be sub-centimetre.
  • Bottom of the column ($z = 2$ m) is 14–25 damping-depths down — the diurnal wave is essentially gone (amplitude $\sim e^{-14} \approx 10^{-6}$), so a coarse cell is harmless.

A uniform 0.5 cm grid would need 400 cells to span 2 m. A uniform 30 cm grid would have only 7 cells but smear the surface signal completely. Geometric stretching gives you both — fine where it matters, coarse where it does not. 24–30 cells total, ~16× savings in computational cost relative to uniform-fine. This is standard practice in land-surface schemes (Best et al. 2011; Masson 2000).

Two consequences that matter downstream.

  1. The FTCS stability bound is set by the smallest $\Delta z^{2}/\kappa$ in the column — which is the top cell of the highest-$\kappa$ layer. The 17 / 33 / 216 second critical time steps cited throughout the walkthrough are all determined by one cell — the top cell — not by the column as a whole.
  2. The harmonic-mean conductivity at faces ($\lambda_{j+1/2}$) is a simplification that strictly assumes equal cell thickness. The general weighted form for unequal cells is $\lambda_{\rm eff} = (\Delta z_1 + \Delta z_2)/(\Delta z_1/\lambda_1 + \Delta z_2/\lambda_2)$, which is what run_experiments.py actually uses. See Section 6.2 of this walkthrough.

How did we land on $\Delta z_0 = 0.5$ cm for the concrete roof? — The four constraints behind the choice

The value 0.5 cm is not arbitrary or "small enough to look fine". It is the result of four overlapping constraints, all of which the top-cell thickness must satisfy at once. Below is each constraint in turn, with the concrete numbers.

Constraint 1 — Resolve the diurnal damping depth. The daily wave decays exponentially with depth $z$, with characteristic length $d$. For the concrete roof, $d = 14.02$ cm. The rule of thumb (consistent with the Nyquist principle for resolving a wave) is that the top grid cell should be at least 8–10× thinner than $d$:

$$\Delta z_0 \;\lesssim\; \frac{d}{10} \;=\; \frac{14.02\text{ cm}}{10} \;\approx\; 1.4\text{ cm}.$$

So any $\Delta z_0 \le 1.4$ cm satisfies this constraint. With $\Delta z_0 = 0.5$ cm, we have $d/\Delta z_0 = 28$ — a generous safety margin (explained immediately below). The amplitude variation across the cell drops to just 3.6% (100% at the top → 96.5% at the bottom), so the cell's single stored temperature is a faithful average. Constraint 1 alone would allow 1 cm too.

What does $d/\Delta z_0$ actually measure, and why call it a "safety margin"?

The ratio $d/\Delta z_0$ is one of the most useful diagnostic numbers in numerical grid design. It deserves its own explanation.

What it physically means. The ratio $d/\Delta z_0$ tells you how many top-cell-widths fit inside one damping depth. Equivalently, it tells you how many grid cells the model devotes to representing one "characteristic length" of the diurnal wave (the depth at which the wave drops to 37% of surface amplitude). For concrete roof:

$$\frac{d}{\Delta z_0} \;=\; \frac{14.02 \text{ cm}}{0.50 \text{ cm}} \;=\; 28.$$

So the model has 28 top-cell-widths of resolution per damping depth on the concrete roof. Whether 28 is "a lot" or "barely enough" depends on the rule of thumb you compare against — that is exactly where the "safety margin" framing comes from.

Why call this a "margin". Numerical-PDE textbooks (and the wave-sampling analogue from signal processing) say: to faithfully resolve an exponentially-decaying or oscillating field with characteristic length $d$, you need at least 8–10 grid cells per $d$. This is the discrete-grid cousin of the Nyquist sampling rule (need at least two samples per wavelength). So the minimum acceptable value of $d/\Delta z_0$ is roughly 8–10.

Our concrete-roof value is 28. The gap between "minimum acceptable" (~10) and "what we actually use" (28) is the margin. We are sampling the wave roughly 3× more finely than the rule of thumb prescribes. If anything goes slightly wrong (a sharper conductivity jump than expected, slightly more diffusive substrate, an unexpected vertical structure), we have headroom before the resolution becomes inadequate.

Why this metric is useful in practice.

  • It directly predicts the amplitude variation across the top cell. If $d/\Delta z_0 = R$, then within a top cell of width $\Delta z_0$, the amplitude factor varies by approximately $1 - e^{-1/R}$. For $R = 28$: $1 - e^{-1/28} = 1 - 0.965 = 3.5\%$. That is exactly the "100% → 96.5%" number we have been quoting for concrete roof. For $R = 8$ (the rule-of-thumb minimum), the variation would be $1 - e^{-1/8} = 11.8\%$ — borderline OK. For $R = 2$ (badly under-resolved), it would be $1 - e^{-1/2} = 39\%$ — disaster.
  • It is dimensionless, so it works across substrates with different $\kappa$. You can compare resolution across asphalt, concrete, and soil with a single number.
  • Diminishing returns past ~15–20. Doubling $d/\Delta z_0$ from 8 to 16 cuts the across-cell amplitude variation from 11.8% to 6%. Doubling again from 16 to 32 cuts it only from 6% to 3%. Past about 15–20 you are paying compute cost for marginal accuracy gains. So the "right" range is roughly 10–20; we sit at 28 for concrete, which is on the generous side but not wasteful.

Calling it a "safety margin" means: how much resolution we have in reserve beyond the minimum the physics demands. The concrete-roof value of 28 is generous; the bare-soil value of 8 is acceptable; anything below ~5 would be a problem.

Constraint 2 — Resolve the topmost layer (the concrete deck itself). The concrete roof column has a 10 cm thick concrete deck on top, then a sharp jump to mineral-wool insulation. To compute the temperature gradient inside the deck accurately, we need at least 5–10 grid cells within that 10 cm layer. With $\Delta z_0 = 0.5$ cm and stretch ratio $r = 1.18$, the first ten cells have total thickness:

$$\Delta z_0 \cdot \frac{r^{10} - 1}{r - 1} \;=\; 0.5 \cdot \frac{1.18^{10} - 1}{0.18} \;\approx\; 0.5 \cdot 23.5 \;\approx\; 11.7\text{ cm}.$$

So roughly 9 cells fit inside the 10 cm concrete deck, which is enough to resolve the gradient there. If we had instead chosen $\Delta z_0 = 1$ cm with $r = 1.18$, the first 10 cells would span 23.4 cm — only about 6 cells would fit inside the 10 cm deck, marginal. Constraint 2 is what kills the otherwise-attractive choice of 1 cm.

Constraint 3 — Resolve the conductivity jump at the deck/insulation interface ($z = 10$ cm). At that interface, $\lambda$ drops from 1.50 to 0.04 — a factor of $\approx 37\times$. The temperature gradient on the insulation side is therefore much steeper than on the deck side, and the harmonic-mean rule (Section 6.2) needs fine cells on both sides of the interface to reproduce that gradient kink. With the stretched grid, by $j = 9$ we are at $\Delta z_j \approx 2.2$ cm — fine enough to resolve the kink. With $\Delta z_0 = 1$ cm, the cells near $z = 10$ cm would be $\approx 5$ cm thick — too coarse to capture the sharp gradient transition. This is the constraint that uniquely picks 0.5 cm over 1 cm for the concrete roof.

Constraint 4 — Stay consistent with standard land-surface-scheme practice. The report (§2.2) cites Best et al. (2011, JULES) and Masson (2000, TEB) as references for the stretched-grid choice. Both use top cells in the 0.5–1 cm range for similar physics. Using values inside that community-accepted range means our solver's numerics can be compared on equal footing with those operational schemes — and that any error we find is attributable to the scheme choice (FTCS / BTCS / CN), not to an idiosyncratic grid.

For asphalt (5 cm top layer), the layer-thickness constraint tightens us to 0.5 cm. For the concrete roof (10 cm deck + 37× $\lambda$ jump), the conductivity-interface constraint locks us to 0.5 cm.

Where does the "37% of surface amplitude" come from?

The formula $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ by itself doesn't mention 37%. The 37% comes from the analytical solution of the heat equation that defines $d$ in the first place. Here is the full story in plain language.

Step 1 — The analytical solution. If the surface temperature is a clean sinusoid $T_s(0, t) = \bar{T} + A\cos(\omega t)$ and the substrate is uniform and infinitely deep, the heat equation has a known closed-form solution (we derive it from scratch in Part 8.1):

$$T_s(z, t) \;=\; \bar{T} \;+\; \underbrace{A \, e^{-z/d}}_{\text{amplitude envelope}} \, \cos\!\bigl(\omega t - z/d\bigr).$$

Two depth-dependent factors are at play: an exponential envelope $A \, e^{-z/d}$ that shrinks the amplitude as you go down, and a phase shift $-z/d$ inside the cosine that delays the wave with depth. The 37% rule is entirely about the first factor — the exponential envelope.

Step 2 — Plug in $z = d$. What is the amplitude when we go down by exactly $z = d$ (one damping-depth)? Substitute into the envelope:

$$A \cdot e^{-d/d} \;=\; A \cdot e^{-1} \;=\; A \cdot \frac{1}{e} \;\approx\; A \cdot 0.3679.$$

So at depth $z = d$, the daily temperature swing is about 37% of the swing at the surface — 0.3679 to be more precise. That number 0.3679 is just $1/e$, where $e \approx 2.718$ is Euler's number. The constant 0.3679 has nothing to do with this project; it is a mathematical property of the exponential function. The reason $1/e$ shows up here is that this is how $d$ was defined: $d$ is the length scale where the exponent equals $-1$.

Step 3 — Why this particular length scale? Look at the algebra in Step 1 again. The complex wavenumber that comes out of plugging $A \, e^{i(\omega t - K z)}$ into the heat equation works out to $K = (1 - i)/d$ with $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$. The $1$ part of $(1 - i)$ gives the cosine; the $-i$ part gives the decay. The grouping of $\kappa$ and $\omega$ inside the square root is forced by the PDE — no other combination has the right units of length. So $d$ is not a free choice; it is the only length scale the heat equation gives you for sinusoidal forcing at frequency $\omega$.

What "amplitude" means in the 37% statement. The amplitude of a sinusoid is half the peak-to-trough swing. If the surface temperature swings between 18 °C and 32 °C (peak-to-trough = 14 K), its amplitude is 7 K. At depth $z = d$, the swing is $0.37 \times 14 = 5.2$ K peak-to-trough, or amplitude $\approx 2.6$ K. So a top surface that varies 25 °C → 47 °C → 25 °C over a day might, at depth $d$, vary only 33 °C → 41 °C → 33 °C. Same average, much narrower swing.

What happens at deeper $z$? The envelope keeps falling exponentially:

  • At $z = 2d$ (two damping-depths): amplitude $= A \cdot e^{-2} \approx 0.135 A$ — about 13.5% of the surface swing.
  • At $z = 3d$: amplitude $= A \cdot e^{-3} \approx 0.050 A$ — about 5%.
  • At $z = 4d$: amplitude $= A \cdot e^{-4} \approx 0.018 A$ — under 2%, essentially zero.

This is why we say "the diurnal signal does not reach 2 m". With our substrates having $d$ in the 8–14 cm range, by 2 m depth we are at $z/d \approx 14$–$25$, where $e^{-14}$ is microscopic. The deep-bottom Neumann zero-flux boundary condition is justified by exactly this calculation.

The single intuition to take away. $d$ is the natural length yardstick of the heat equation for daily-cycle forcing — and what makes it natural is that the amplitude falls by exactly a factor of $1/e \approx 37\%$ for each unit of $z$ measured in $d$'s. The 37% is a definition, not a measurement; the formula $d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}$ tells you how big that yardstick is for each substrate, not what fraction the wave is at depth $d$. Those are two separate pieces of information bolted together in the phrase "the depth at which the daily temperature wave decays to 37%".

1.5 The four-component surface energy balance

Surface temperature $T_s^0$ is determined by an energy balance at the surface. Four fluxes meet there:

  • $R_n$ — net absorbed radiation, positive when energy is being deposited.
  • $H$ — sensible heat flux to the air, positive when heat leaves the surface upward.
  • $LE$ — latent heat flux from evaporation. In this project $LE = 0$.
  • $G$ — conductive heat flux into the substrate (positive downward).

Energy conservation requires what arrives equals what leaves:

$$R_n - H - LE - G = 0.$$

Status: [Established] — first law of thermodynamics applied at the surface.

Reading this equation. Four quantities all in W/m². $R_n$ (read "R-sub-n", short for "net radiation") is energy coming in from sun and sky. $H$, $LE$, $G$ are three pathways energy leaves the surface. Conservation: incoming = sum of outgoing, or equivalently $R_n - H - LE - G = 0$. This is the surface energy balance (SEB).

The notation $T_s^0$ means "$T$ at the substrate surface, depth zero". The "0" superscript is a label (depth $z = 0$), not an exponent.

1.6 Units and dimensional analysis sanity check

What we are doing in this subsection. Every term in every equation we will write has a physical meaning and physical units. Mixing them up is the most common error in coupled physics-code: a missing factor of $\rho$ or a stray $\Delta z$ destroys energy conservation silently. Below we list every key term, what it physically means, the intuition for it, and a unit check that proves the equation is self-consistent. Treat this as a "decoder ring" you can come back to whenever a symbol stops making sense.

1. The heat-storage term: $C \, \partial T/\partial t$ — units (J/m³/K)·(K/s) = W/m³.

  • What it means physically. The rate at which one cubic metre of substrate is storing (or releasing) thermal energy as its temperature changes.
  • Intuition. $C$ is "how much energy fits in this material per °C of warming". Multiply by "how fast the temperature is rising" ($\partial T/\partial t$, K/s) and you get "how fast energy is piling up per cubic metre" (W/m³). The term is positive when the substrate is heating up (absorbing energy) and negative when it is cooling (releasing it).
  • Unit check. (J/m³/K)(K/s) — the Kelvin cancels and we are left with J/(m³·s) = W/m³. ✓

2. The divergence-of-flux term: $\partial/\partial z [\lambda \, \partial T/\partial z]$ — units W/m³.

  • What it means physically. The rate at which heat flux is changing with depth — equivalently, the net heat being deposited (per cubic metre) at a given level because more is flowing in from above than flowing out below.
  • Intuition. Inside the brackets, $\lambda \, \partial T/\partial z$ is Fourier's law: thermal conductivity times temperature gradient. Take the depth derivative of that, and you ask "is the heat flux speeding up or slowing down as I go down?" If flux entering a thin slab from above exceeds flux leaving the bottom, energy is accumulating inside the slab — that accumulation rate, in W/m³, is what this term measures.
  • Unit check. Inside the brackets: (W/m/K)·(K/m) = W/m². Outside: divide by an additional m → W/m³. ✓ Notice it matches the storage term — that match is exactly what makes the heat equation a statement of energy conservation.

3. The diffusion number: $\nu = \kappa \Delta t / \Delta z^{2}$ — dimensionless.

  • What it means physically. The ratio of two times: $\Delta t$ (one numerical time step) divided by the diffusion time across one grid cell ($\Delta z^{2}/\kappa$). If $\nu = 1$, in one time step heat diffuses across exactly one cell. If $\nu = 0.5$, it diffuses across half a cell. If $\nu = 10$, the model is asking diffusion to leap across ten cells in one step — which is unphysical and breaks explicit schemes.
  • Intuition. $\nu$ is the single most important number in stability analysis. It does not matter whether you change $\kappa$, $\Delta t$, or $\Delta z$ — only the combination $\kappa \Delta t / \Delta z^{2}$ controls whether FTCS lives or dies. That's why we give it a name.
  • Unit check. (m²/s)·(s)/m² — everything cancels. ✓

4. The aerodynamic resistance: $r_a = 1/(C_H U)$ — units s/m.

  • What it means physically. How hard it is, in a turbulent sense, for heat to move from the surface up into the air. Big $r_a$ → calm, stratified air, weak surface-air coupling. Small $r_a$ → windy, turbulent air, strong coupling.
  • Intuition. Think of $r_a$ as an electrical resistance and the temperature difference $T_s^0 - T_a$ as a voltage; sensible heat flux $H$ is the resulting current. The denominator $C_H U$ is bigger when the wind blows harder (eddies carry heat more efficiently) or when the surface is rougher (more turbulence). So $r_a$ falls and $H$ rises. The strict-impervious project uses constants $C_H = 5\times 10^{-3}$ and $U = 3$ m/s, giving $r_a = 1/(0.005 \cdot 3) = 66.7$ s/m.
  • Unit check. $C_H$ is dimensionless, $U$ is m/s, so $C_H U$ has units m/s. Its reciprocal is s/m. ✓

5. The sensible heat flux: $H = \rho c_p (T_s^0 - T_a)/r_a$ — units W/m².

  • What it means physically. The rate (per square metre of surface) at which the surface dumps heat into the air via turbulent eddies. Daytime: $T_s^0 > T_a$ so $H > 0$ — surface heats the air. Nighttime: usually $T_s^0 < T_a$ so $H < 0$ — air warms the surface slightly.
  • Intuition. The numerator $\rho c_p (T_s^0 - T_a)$ is "how much energy difference per cubic metre of air exists between the surface and the reference level". Divide by $r_a$ (seconds per metre) and you get joules per cubic metre per (seconds per metre) = J/(m²·s) = W/m². The whole formula is the thermal-electrical analogue: $H = (\text{voltage})/(\text{resistance})$ with voltage = temperature difference scaled by air's heat capacity, resistance = $r_a$.
  • Unit check. (kg/m³)(J/kg/K)(K)/(s/m) = (J/m³)/(s/m) = J·m/(m³·s) = J/(m²·s) = W/m². ✓

The dimensional moral. Look at the storage term (W/m³) and the divergence-of-flux term (W/m³). They match. That is not a coincidence — that match is the heat equation. Now look at $R_n$, $H$, $LE$, $G$ in Section 1.5: all W/m². They also match. That is the surface energy balance. Whenever you write a new equation in this project, run a unit check first: if the units don't agree, the equation is wrong, regardless of how plausible it looks.

2.1 What is a partial differential equation?

What we are doing in this subsection. The heat-conduction equation involves derivatives in both time and space, which makes it a partial differential equation (PDE) rather than an ordinary one. The next few paragraphs explain what that means and why the operator $\partial^2 T/\partial z^2$ — the second spatial derivative — is the geometric mechanism that makes heat diffuse.

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) describes how something changes with respect to one variable. Newton's law of cooling, $dT/dt = -k(T - T_\infty)$, is an ODE.

A partial differential equation (PDE) involves derivatives with respect to more than one variable. Heat conduction in depth is a PDE because $T$ depends on both $z$ and $t$:

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}.$$

Status: [Established] — the diffusion equation. Re-derived in Part 6.1.

Reading this equation. The symbol $\partial$ (a curly d, read "partial") is used instead of regular $d$ when the function depends on multiple variables. So $\partial T/\partial t$ means "rate of change of $T$ with respect to $t$, holding $z$ fixed". $\partial^2 T/\partial z^2$ is the second partial derivative with respect to $z$ — the curvature of $T$ as a function of $z$ at a fixed time.

In plain words: at every point $(z, t)$, the temperature is rising in time at a rate proportional to how curved it is in space at that moment. If $T$ is concave-up (curving upward like a bowl bottom), the second derivative is positive and $T$ rises with time. If $T$ is concave-down (peak), $T$ falls. So heat diffuses by smoothing out curvature: peaks decay, troughs fill in.

Wrapping up Section 2.1. The single most useful image: heat diffusion is the universe pressing iron on wrinkled fabric — high curvature is flattened, every peak relaxes toward its neighbours. With that picture established, the next subsection labels the heat equation by its family ("parabolic"), which tells us what kind of stability and accuracy issues to expect.

2.2 The PDE classification — why heat is parabolic

What we are doing in this subsection. Different PDEs behave differently — some propagate waves (hyperbolic), some smooth disturbances away (parabolic), some have no time direction at all (elliptic). Knowing which family our equation belongs to tells us in advance what to worry about. For the heat equation we will confirm it is parabolic, which means: there is no wave-like propagation, only diffusive smoothing, and the maximum stable explicit time step shrinks as $\Delta z^2$ — that is the famous restrictive bound of FTCS.

Notes #2 classifies linear second-order PDEs by the discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ of the general form

$$A u_{xx} + B u_{xy} + C u_{yy} + \text{lower-order terms} = 0.$$

Status: [Established] — standard PDE classification.

Reading this equation. Shorthand: $u_{xx}$ means $\partial^2 u/\partial x^2$, $u_{xy}$ means $\partial^2 u/\partial x \partial y$, $u_{yy}$ means $\partial^2 u/\partial y^2$. $A$, $B$, $C$ are coefficient functions. The discriminant $B^2 - 4AC$ is the same algebraic combination that classifies conic sections (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola).

  • Elliptic ($B^2 - 4AC < 0$). Steady-state, no time direction.
  • Hyperbolic ($B^2 - 4AC > 0$). Wave-like.
  • Parabolic ($B^2 - 4AC = 0$). Diffusion-like.

Our heat conduction equation $u_t - \kappa u_{zz} = 0$ has $A = -\kappa$ for $u_{zz}$, with $B = 0$ and $C = 0$. Discriminant: $0 - 4(-\kappa)(0) = 0$ — parabolic.

What "parabolic" tells us in practice. Three things. (1) Diffusion-only: no information propagates as a wave. (2) Stiff at fine grids: an explicit time step must shrink as $\Delta z^2$, which is brutal at sub-centimetre resolution. (3) Implicit-friendly: backward and trapezoidal schemes are unconditionally stable on parabolic equations. So just by classifying the PDE we already know which numerical recipes will work and which will not.

2.3 The two basic finite-difference ideas

What we are doing in this subsection. A computer cannot evaluate $\partial T/\partial z$ exactly — it only stores temperature at a few discrete depths. We need approximate formulas that turn a derivative into a difference of two (or three) stored numbers. Taylor's theorem gives us those formulas. By the end of this subsection you will know the three workhorses: forward-difference, backward-difference, and centred-difference for first derivatives, and the centred three-point formula for second derivatives. Every line of the project's solver is built from these four formulas.

For a smooth function $f(x)$, Taylor's theorem gives:

$$f(x + \Delta x) = f(x) + \Delta x f'(x) + \frac{\Delta x^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{\Delta x^3}{6} f'''(x) + O(\Delta x^4).$$

Status: [Established] — Taylor's theorem.

Reading this equation. Left side: value of $f$ at a slightly different point. Right side: an expression using only $f$ and its derivatives at the original point $x$. Notation: $f'(x)$ is the first derivative, $f''(x)$ the second, $f'''(x)$ the third. $O(\Delta x^4)$ means "all remaining terms are of order $\Delta x^4$ or smaller". So if $\Delta x$ is small, knowing $f$ and its derivatives at $x$ lets us approximate $f(x + \Delta x)$.

Rearranging gives three estimates of $f'(x)$:

Forward difference:

$$f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x}.$$

Status: [Established] — first-order accurate, error $O(\Delta x)$.

Reading this equation. Take Taylor's series, subtract $f(x)$ from both sides, divide by $\Delta x$. Get $f'(x) + (\Delta x/2) f''(x) + \ldots$. Drop everything but the first term. The dropped term is order $\Delta x$ — the error. In plain words: take the slope between this point and the next one and call it the derivative.

Backward difference:

$$f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x) - f(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x}.$$

Status: [Established] — first-order.

Centred difference:

$$f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x - \Delta x)}{2 \Delta x}.$$

Status: [Established] — second-order, $O(\Delta x^2)$.

Reading this equation. Average the slopes from this point to the next and from this point to the previous, and call it the derivative. When we subtract the forward and backward Taylor expansions, the $f''$ terms have opposite signs and cancel. First surviving error is $O(\Delta x^2)$.

For a second derivative we use the centred three-point formula:

$$f''(x) \approx \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - 2f(x) + f(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x^2} + O(\Delta x^2).$$

Status: [Established] — second-order accurate.

Reading this equation. "Forward slope at this point" minus "backward slope at this point", divided by $\Delta x$. That gives "rate of change of slope" = second derivative.

Wrapping up Section 2.3. Four formulas in your toolbox now: three for $f'(x)$ (forward, backward, centred) and one for $f''(x)$ (centred three-point). Every PDE we numerically solve in this project is built from combinations of these. Centred-in-space gives second-order accuracy "for free" by symmetric cancellation. Forward-in-time will deliver FTCS, backward-in-time will deliver BTCS, and a symmetric weighted average of the two will deliver Crank–Nicolson — Part 3 puts the pieces together.

2.4 What does "stability" mean?

What we are doing in this subsection. Accuracy and stability are different things. A scheme can be very accurate per step but still blow up over many steps if errors compound; a different scheme can have larger per-step error but stay bounded forever. We need precise language for the difference, because the project's whole punchline is "FTCS is plenty accurate per step but conditionally stable, so it cannot be used at operational time steps; BTCS and CN trade some per-step accuracy for unconditional stability."

A finite-difference scheme is stable if small numerical errors at one time step do not grow unboundedly over many time steps. If errors grow, the solution "blows up" — temperatures become infinite or NaN. Stability is separate from accuracy.

Some schemes are conditionally stable — $\Delta t$ must be smaller than some bound. Others are unconditionally stable.

Part 3. The vocabulary of finite-difference time-stepping schemes

Why this part exists — and what you will get from it

Part 2 gave us four finite-difference formulas for derivatives. Part 3 chooses which of those formulas to apply to the time derivative. There are only three really useful choices — and they are not equally good. The whole question of "which time-stepping scheme should I use?" reduces to picking among them.

The three options. (1) Evaluate the rate at the start of the interval (FTCS, $\alpha = 0$): cheap but only conditionally stable. (2) Evaluate the rate at the end (BTCS, $\alpha = 1$): expensive (implicit solve) but always stable. (3) Average start and end (Crank–Nicolson, $\alpha = 1/2$): same cost as BTCS but second-order accurate. All three are special cases of a single formula — the $\theta$-method.

How this connects to the project. Every result in Section 4 of the long report is one of "FTCS at $\alpha = 0$" or "BTCS at $\alpha = 1$" or "CN at $\alpha = 1/2$" applied to the substrate column. The 27-cell experiment matrix in the long report is built around varying $\alpha$ (three choices) × substrate (three choices) × $\Delta t$ (three choices). So Part 3 is the parameterisation that organises the entire experimental design.

3.1 The general framework

What we are doing in this subsection. We introduce a notation that lets us write all three time-stepping schemes in a single formula. The key idea is that the next value $T^{n+1}$ equals the current value $T^n$ plus the time integral of the rate function $f(T, t)$ over one step. Different ways of approximating that integral give different schemes.

Lecture 6 introduces notation: let $\psi$ denote the true solution, $\phi_j^n$ the numerical approximation at $t = n \Delta t$. For the ODE form $d\psi/dt = f(\psi, t)$, the goal is to construct schemes for advancing $\phi^n \to \phi^{n+1}$ that approximate the exact integral

$$\psi^{n+1} = \psi^n + \int_{n\Delta t}^{(n+1)\Delta t} f(\psi, t) dt.$$

Status: [Established] — fundamental theorem of calculus.

Reading this equation. Symbols: $\psi$ (Greek "psi") is the true solution; $\phi$ (Greek "phi") is the numerical approximation. Superscripts $n$ and $n+1$ are time-level indices (not exponents): $\phi^n$ means "$\phi$ at time $n\Delta t$". The integral on the right is the area under the rate-function curve $f$ from $t = n\Delta t$ to $t = (n+1)\Delta t$. In plain words: the next value equals the current value plus the accumulated change in between, where the change is the integral of the rate $f$.

3.2 Explicit vs implicit (Notes #6 Section 5)

Explicit (forward Euler): $T^{n+1} = T^n + \Delta t f(T^n)$.

Reading this scheme. The new value $T^{n+1}$ equals the old value $T^n$ plus $\Delta t$ times the rate $f$ at the old time level. We approximate the integral as "(rate at start of interval) × (interval length)". Cheap per step, conditionally stable.

Implicit (backward Euler): $T^{n+1} = T^n + \Delta t f(T^{n+1})$.

Reading this scheme. Same form, but $f$ is evaluated at the new time level $T^{n+1}$. Since $T^{n+1}$ appears on both sides, we have to solve an algebraic equation. Expensive per step, unconditionally stable.

Status: [Established] schemes — both date from Euler.

3.3 The trapezoidal rule = Crank–Nicolson

Take the average of explicit and implicit:

$$T^{n+1} = T^n + \frac{\Delta t}{2}[f(T^n) + f(T^{n+1})].$$

Status: [Established] — trapezoidal rule for ODE integration. Applied to the heat equation it is named Crank–Nicolson (1947).

Reading this scheme. Approximate the integral as the average of rate at start and rate at end, times interval length. Geometrically a trapezoid under the rate curve. The symmetric averaging cancels the leading first-order error term — second-order accurate.

3.4 The full $\theta$-method family

What we are doing in this subsection. We compress FTCS, BTCS, and CN into a single formula governed by one parameter, $\alpha$ (also called $\theta$ in some textbooks). This is the cornerstone of the project's code architecture: there is one Python routine, and it takes $\alpha$ as an argument. Set $\alpha = 0$ and the routine becomes FTCS; set $\alpha = 1$ and it becomes BTCS; set $\alpha = 1/2$ and it becomes Crank–Nicolson. One implementation, three schemes — debugged once, verified once, ready to run hundreds of experiments without code duplication.

All three schemes are special cases of one formula:

$$T^{n+1} = T^n + \Delta t [\alpha f(T^{n+1}) + (1-\alpha) f(T^n)].$$

Status: [Established] — the $\theta$-method.

Reading this equation, the easy way. "The new temperature equals the old temperature plus a step of length $\Delta t$, where the step is a weighted average of two rates: the rate at the new (still-unknown) value with weight $\alpha$, and the rate at the old (known) value with weight $(1-\alpha)$." Three intuitive special cases:

  • $\alpha = 0$ (FTCS). All weight on the old rate. Cheap (just plug in known numbers and compute). Conditionally stable: must obey $\nu \le 1/2$. The numerical equivalent of "look at the current slope, walk a step in that direction".
  • $\alpha = 1$ (BTCS). All weight on the new rate. Implicit (you must solve an equation, because the new rate depends on the unknown new value). Unconditionally stable, first-order. The equivalent of "look at the slope at the destination, walk so the slope-of-arrival is consistent".
  • $\alpha = 1/2$ (Crank–Nicolson). Average old and new rates. Implicit, but second-order accurate. The equivalent of "use the average of the slope at the start and the slope at the end" — a trapezoidal rule under the rate curve, which by symmetric cancellation kills the leading first-order error.
$\alpha$ Scheme Order in $\Delta t$ Stability
$0$ FTCS / Forward Euler first conditional, $\nu \le 1/2$
$1/2$ Crank–Nicolson second unconditional
$1$ BTCS / Backward Euler first unconditional

Lecture 6 introduces these three schemes by name. The lecture-note formulae are

$$\phi^{n+1} = \phi^n + \Delta t f^n \quad \text{(Euler/forward)}$$

$$\phi^{n+1} = \phi^n + \Delta t f^{n+1} \quad \text{(Backward)}$$

$$\phi^{n+1} = \phi^n + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t (f^n + f^{n+1}) \quad \text{(Trapezoidal)}$$

3.5 Other schemes you have seen

Notes #6 discusses several more schemes.

Matsuno scheme (predictor-corrector):

$$\phi^{* n+1} = \phi^n + \Delta t f^n, \qquad \phi^{n+1} = \phi^n + \Delta t f(\phi^{* n+1}).$$

Reading this scheme. Two stages. The asterisk in $\phi^*$ flags it as a "guess" or "predictor". First a forward Euler step to get $\phi^*$. Then re-evaluate $f$ at the predicted value $\phi^*$, and use that for the actual update. Iterative explicit; approximates Backward Euler.

Heun scheme (2nd-order Runge–Kutta): explicit, second-order.

Leapfrog: $\phi^{n+1} = \phi^{n-1} + 2\Delta t f^n$. Three-level, explicit, second-order. Unstable for diffusion.

[HW3] is the direct ancestor:

  • Case 1 (Euler): amplitude grows above 1 — unstable.
  • Case 2 (Backward): amplitude decays below 1 — stable but damping.
  • Case 3 (Trapezoidal): amplitude stays at 1 — neutrally stable.
  • Case 4 (Matsuno): amplitude decays slightly.

3.6 Why the project uses the $\theta$-method specifically — and why not something else

What we are doing in this subsection. We make explicit the reasoning behind one of the project's most important architectural choices. The $\theta$-method is not the only option — we could have used a Runge–Kutta family, a leapfrog, a Matsuno predictor-corrector, an exponential integrator, or many others. We picked the $\theta$-method on purpose. Below is the full justification, with each alternative answered.

The three concrete benefits of the $\theta$-method for this project

  1. One implementation, three schemes. A single function step_alpha(T, dt, ..., alpha, ...) covers FTCS, BTCS, and CN. We debug once, verify once, and the comparison across schemes is mechanical — there is no risk that "FTCS uses a different harmonic mean than CN" because the code path is identical. This matters because the project's central contribution is a controlled comparison: same physics, same grid, same boundary conditions, only $\alpha$ varies.
  2. Reuses the same linear solver. When $\alpha > 0$, the right-hand side depends on $T^{n+1}$ at each cell and its two neighbours, producing a banded-tridiagonal matrix. The same SciPy banded solver (scipy.linalg.solve_banded) handles BTCS, CN, and any intermediate $\alpha$ — $O(N)$ cost per step. Adding a 4th-order Runge–Kutta or a Magnus integrator would require a separate solver path and three times the testing surface.
  3. Stability regions are textbook-known. The von Neumann analysis we walk through in Section 6.5 is documented in every numerical-PDE textbook. We did not have to derive new stability conditions — we are reusing a stability theory that has been used for 70+ years. That predictability is itself a deliverable: when we report that FTCS blows up at the predicted moment in Section 8.1, the prediction comes from this well-established theory.

Why $\theta$-method, not the alternatives

  • vs. fully explicit Runge–Kutta (RK4). RK4 is fourth-order in time and entirely explicit. But for diffusion, RK4 still has a stability bound proportional to $\Delta z^{2}/\kappa$ — only slightly more permissive than FTCS. We would have to take very small time steps for any of the cell sizes we care about. The unconditional stability of BTCS/CN is what really lets us reach 600 s steps.
  • vs. leapfrog (centred-in-time, centred-in-space). Leapfrog is second-order accurate, simple, and beloved in atmospheric dynamics. But it is unconditionally unstable for pure diffusion, regardless of $\Delta t$ — the amplification factor at the $2\Delta z$ wave has $|A| > 1$ for any $\nu > 0$. The Notes #9 (Pure Diffusion) call this out explicitly. So leapfrog is the wrong family for our parabolic problem.
  • vs. Matsuno (predictor-corrector). Matsuno is two-stage explicit; we covered it in §3.5. It approximates Backward Euler at the cost of two right-hand-side evaluations per step. Compared to BTCS proper, Matsuno is slightly less stable (still conditionally bounded) and not noticeably more accurate. The implicit BTCS, especially with the tridiagonal solver, wins both on stability and on cost amortised over the 27-cell experiment matrix.
  • vs. exponential integrators. Exponential time-differencing (e.g., ETD-RK4) achieves both high order and unconditional stability for stiff linear problems. They are excellent for problems with a clean linear part — but our SEB is nonlinear (the $T_{s}^{4}$ longwave term), so the exponential is no longer of a closed-form linear operator. The added implementation complexity is not justified by the modest accuracy gain at $\Delta t \le 600$ s.
  • vs. higher-order BDF (BDF3, BDF4). Backward differentiation formulas of order 3+ are also unconditionally stable for diffusion, but multi-step (need history). For a project meant to be didactic and easy to verify, the simplicity of one-step methods wins.

Why $\theta$-method is especially handy in this project

The project's headline experiment is to compare FTCS, BTCS, and CN at three different time steps on three different substrates. With the $\theta$-method, each of those 27 runs is one call to the same Python function with three arguments changed. The cleanliness of the experimental design — and therefore the credibility of the headline numerical results (40% over-amplification on BTCS, halved by CN) — is a direct consequence of using a single parameterised method instead of three independent codes. If we had implemented FTCS, BTCS, and CN as three separate routines, the comparison would have been muddied by tiny implementation differences and we would never know whether the 40% number is "real" or an artefact.

5.3 What "wavenumber-dependent damping" means

What we are doing in this subsection. The introduction uses the phrase "wavenumber-dependent damping" without unpacking it. We unpack it here so that when the von Neumann analysis arrives in Part 6.5, you already know what it is about to compute.

Every numerical scheme can be characterised by how it changes the amplitude of each Fourier component (each wavelength) per time step. We will derive these amplification factors explicitly in Part 6.5.

The single intuition to take away. Imagine the substrate's temperature profile as a stack of sinusoidal waves at different vertical wavelengths. Slide 4 — Every equation on the slide, explained as easily as possible

Slide 4 has four mathematical objects (one governing equation, one harmonic-mean rule, one θ-method update equation, and one boundary-conditions table) plus a small column diagram. Each one looks intimidating at first glance. Below is the simplest possible reading of each.

Equation 1 — The governing heat equation.

$$C_s(z) \, \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\left[ \lambda_s(z) \, \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z} \right].$$

What the symbols are. $T_s(z, t)$ is substrate temperature at depth $z$ and time $t$; $C_s(z)$ is the heat capacity at that depth (J/m³/K); $\lambda_s(z)$ is the thermal conductivity at that depth (W/m/K).

What each side says. The left side asks "how fast is energy being stored per cubic metre at this depth?" Heat capacity $C_s$ times rate-of-temperature-change $\partial T_s/\partial t$ gives the answer in W/m³. The right side asks "what is the net heat flux arriving at this depth per cubic metre?" Inside the bracket is Fourier's law $\lambda \, \partial T/\partial z$ — heat flux. Taking the $z$-derivative of that flux asks "is heat flux speeding up or slowing down with depth?" If more flux enters from above than leaves below, energy piles up.

The simplest possible reading. "Temperature rises at a rate equal to how much heat is being shoved into this slab from above minus how much is escaping out the bottom." That is energy conservation, in one line.

Why conductivity form, not diffusivity form. If $\lambda$ and $C$ were constants, we could pull them outside and write $\partial T/\partial t = \kappa \, \partial^{2} T/\partial z^{2}$ — the "diffusivity form" you see in textbooks. But the urban substrates we care about have layers (asphalt over aggregate over soil), so $\lambda$ and $C$ jump across interfaces. Keeping them inside the derivative preserves discrete heat conservation across those jumps. That is why every line of run_experiments.py uses the conductivity form.

Equation 2 — The harmonic-mean rule for $\lambda$ at faces.

$$\lambda_{j+1/2} \;=\; \frac{2\,\lambda_j\,\lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j + \lambda_{j+1}}.$$

What the symbols are. $\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_{j+1}$ are conductivities at two adjacent cell centres; $\lambda_{j+1/2}$ is the effective conductivity at the face between them (the place where flux is evaluated).

The simplest possible reading. "Two materials in series resist heat flow the way two resistors in series resist current — the effective conductivity is dominated by the worst (lowest-$\lambda$) one." If $\lambda_1 = 1.5$ (concrete) and $\lambda_2 = 0.04$ (insulation), the arithmetic mean would say 0.77 (too high by a factor of 10!). The harmonic mean gives 0.078 — much closer to the insulation value, correctly capturing that the insulator throttles the flux.

Why this matters. Concrete deck sits on insulation in the project's roof substrate. Without the harmonic mean, the model would over-conduct heat across that interface by an order of magnitude.

Equation 3 — The $\theta$-method update.

$$\frac{C_j \Delta z_j \, \bigl(T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n\bigr)}{\Delta t} \;=\; \alpha \, \bigl[-\partial G/\partial z\bigr]^{n+1} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\bigl[-\partial G/\partial z\bigr]^{n}.$$

What the symbols are. $T_j^n$ is the temperature in cell $j$ at time level $n$; $T_j^{n+1}$ is the unknown new value one step later; $C_j \Delta z_j$ is the heat capacity per square metre of horizontal area for cell $j$; $\Delta t$ is the time step; $[-\partial G/\partial z]$ is the negative divergence of the flux (the right-hand-side of the heat equation, evaluated cell-by-cell as $G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}$); $\alpha$ is the implicitness weight.

The simplest possible reading, left side. "Change in stored energy per second." Take the difference between new and old temperature, multiply by the cell's thermal mass per unit area, divide by the elapsed time. That is the energy accumulation rate.

The simplest possible reading, right side. "Net heating rate, evaluated at a weighted average of the new time and the old time." Weight $\alpha$ on the new (still-unknown) time, weight $(1-\alpha)$ on the old (known) time. Setting energy-accumulation rate equal to net heating rate is energy conservation across one step.

Three special cases.

  • $\alpha = 0$ (FTCS): all weight on the old (known) heating rate. Computer-friendly because the right side has only known values — just plug in and compute. But conditionally stable: must obey $\nu \le 1/2$.
  • $\alpha = 1$ (BTCS): all weight on the new heating rate. The right side now depends on the unknown $T_j^{n+1}$ at all cells, so we have to solve a linear system. Unconditionally stable, first-order in time.
  • $\alpha = 1/2$ (Crank–Nicolson): average of old and new heating rates. Same linear-solve cost as BTCS, but second-order accurate in time because the symmetric average kills the leading error term.

The pills on Slide 4 — purple FTCS, teal CN, orange BTCS — are visual reminders that one formula and one Python function cover all three schemes. Change $\alpha$, get a different scheme. That single architectural choice is what makes the 27-cell experiment matrix in Section 4 of the long report a controlled comparison rather than a comparison-of-three-different-implementations.

Boundary conditions on Slide 4 (the BC table). Two BCs are needed because the heat equation is second-order in $z$.

  • Top BC: SEB closure, $R_n - H - LE - G = 0$. At the top cell we don't prescribe $T_s^0$ directly — instead we make the four surface fluxes balance to zero by solving for $T_s^0$ with Newton iteration to $|\Delta T| < 10^{-4}$ K. Why iteration? Because $R_n$ depends on $T_s^0$ via the Stefan–Boltzmann $T^{4}$ term, which is nonlinear.
  • Bottom BC: zero-flux Neumann at $z = 2$ m. Discretely: $T_{N-1} = T_{N-2}$, forcing the gradient at the deepest face to zero. Physically reasonable because the diurnal signal does not reach 2 m depth (damping depth is only 8–14 cm).
  • Forcing: $S_\downarrow$ peaks at 1000 W/m² at noon; $T_a$ at 292.5 K with 7.5 K amplitude peaking at 14:00 LT; $U$ constant at 3 m/s.
  • Order of ops: advance the column with the current $T_s^0$ held fixed, then update $T_s^0$ from the SEB using the new $T_1$. This sequencing is the source of the operator-splitting error that dominates results at coarse $\Delta t$.

6.1 Section 2.1 — Where the heat conduction equation comes from

What we are doing in this subsection. The heat equation looks like a thing to memorise, but it is actually a consequence of two simpler laws: Fourier's law (heat flows from hot to cold proportional to the gradient) and energy conservation (heat does not appear or disappear, it just moves). Combining them gives the conductivity-form heat equation. We do the derivation here so that you understand why it has the structure it does and why "conductivity form" is the right form for layered substrates.

The report writes the heat conduction equation in conductivity form:

$$C_s(z) \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left[\lambda_s(z) \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z}\right].$$

Status: [Derived] below from Fourier's law plus energy conservation.

Reading this equation. Symbol-by-symbol:

  • $T_s(z, t)$ — substrate temperature at depth $z$ and time $t$. The "s" subscript stands for "substrate" (distinguishing from air temperature $T_a$).
  • $z$ — vertical depth, with $z = 0$ at the surface and increasing downward. So $z = 0.05$ means 5 cm below the surface.
  • $t$ — time, in seconds.
  • $\lambda_s(z)$ — thermal conductivity of the substrate at depth $z$. Written with $(z)$ to indicate $\lambda$ can change with depth (because we have layered substrates).
  • $C_s(z)$ — volumetric heat capacity at depth $z$. Same depth-dependence.
  • $\partial T_s/\partial t$ — partial derivative with respect to time, holding $z$ fixed. The rate at which temperature is changing in time at a given depth.
  • $\partial T_s/\partial z$ — partial derivative with respect to depth, holding $t$ fixed. The temperature gradient.
  • The big bracket $\partial/\partial z [ \lambda_s(z) \partial T_s/\partial z ]$ — first compute the temperature gradient, multiply by $\lambda_s$, then take the derivative with respect to $z$ of the result.

What the equation says in plain words. "The volumetric heat capacity multiplied by the rate of temperature change in time equals the spatial divergence of the conductive heat flux." Rearranged: at any depth and time, energy is being deposited locally at a rate equal to how the heat flux changes with depth. If more heat is flowing in from above than flowing out below, energy accumulates and temperature rises.

Derivation step by step

Step 1. Fourier's law of heat conduction.

$$q(z) = -\lambda_s(z) \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z}.$$

Status: [Established] — Fourier's law (Joseph Fourier, 1822).

Reading this equation. Symbols:

  • $q(z)$ — heat flux at depth $z$. Units W/m².
  • $-\lambda_s$ — minus the thermal conductivity.
  • $\partial T_s/\partial z$ — temperature gradient at depth $z$.

In words: heat flux equals the negative of conductivity times temperature gradient. The minus sign encodes the second law: heat flows from hot to cold. If $T$ decreases with depth ($\partial T/\partial z < 0$), then $q = -\lambda \cdot (\text{negative}) > 0$ — flux is in the $+z$ direction (downward). Matches physical intuition.

Step 2. Energy conservation in a thin slab. Pick a thin horizontal slab from depth $z$ to $z + dz$. Energy stored per unit horizontal area in the slab: $C_s T_s dz$. Rate of change of stored energy = net flux into the slab = $q(z) - q(z + dz)$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(C_s T_s dz) = q(z) - q(z + dz).$$

Status: [Established] — first law of thermodynamics applied to a thin slab.

Reading this equation. Left side: rate of change of total energy stored in this slab. Right side: net rate at which heat is flowing into the slab. Energy conservation: they must be equal.

If $C_s$ is independent of time and we Taylor-expand $q(z + dz) \approx q(z) + (\partial q/\partial z) dz$:

$$C_s \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} dz = -\frac{\partial q}{\partial z} dz.$$

Reading this step. Both sides have factor $dz$; divide it out. Substituting Fourier's law $q = -\lambda_s \partial T_s/\partial z$:

$$C_s \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(-\lambda_s \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(\lambda_s \frac{\partial T_s}{\partial z}).$$

That is the conductivity-form heat equation. Status: [Derived] from two established laws.

Step 3. Why "conductivity form" instead of "diffusivity form"? If $\lambda$ and $C$ are constant, we can pull them out: $\partial T/\partial t = \kappa \partial^2 T/\partial z^2$ with $\kappa = \lambda/C$. In our layered substrates, $\lambda$ and $C$ jump across material interfaces, so we keep them inside the derivative to preserve discrete heat conservation.

Wrapping up Section 6.1. You now know where the heat equation comes from (two physical laws plus algebra), which form to use (conductivity form, because of layered materials), and what it physically says (heat content per cell rises when more flux enters from above than leaves below). Section 6.2 chops the depth axis into cells so a computer can evaluate the conductivity form.

6.2 Section 2.2 — The grid, the half-levels, the harmonic mean

What we are doing in this subsection. A computer stores a finite list of temperatures, not a continuous function $T(z, t)$. We need to decide: where on the depth axis do we evaluate temperature, where do we evaluate flux, and how do we handle the material discontinuities (concrete-on-insulation). Three pieces fall out: a staggered grid (temperatures at cell centres, fluxes at faces), a half-level naming convention ($j+1/2$), and the harmonic-mean rule for $\lambda$ at faces. These three pieces together guarantee that the discrete code conserves heat exactly across material jumps.

Cell centres and the staggered grid

Replace continuous depth $z$ with cell centres $z_0, z_1, \ldots, z_{N-1}$. At each cell store one number: temperature $T_j$ at the centre of cell $j$.

Reading the notation. $T_j$ is "the temperature at cell $j$". The subscript $j$ is just a cell index — an integer from 0 up to $N-1$, where $N$ is the total number of cells. Between cells $j$ and $j+1$ lies a face at depth $z_{j+1/2} = (z_j + z_{j+1})/2$. The half-integer subscript $j+1/2$ is read as "between cell $j$ and cell $j+1$" — it is not literally a cell, just a label for the location halfway between two adjacent centres.

This staggered grid is the standard finite-volume layout. Heat that leaves cell $j$ across face $j+1/2$ is the same heat that enters cell $j+1$ across the same face — energy conserved by construction.

[HW5] showed empirically that on the unstaggered grid, a single point disturbance only spread to every other grid point. On the staggered grid, the disturbance spread smoothly.

The discrete flux equation

$$G_{j+1/2} = \lambda_{j+1/2} \frac{T_j - T_{j+1}}{z_{j+1} - z_j}.$$

Status: [Definition] — discretization of Fourier's law at the half-level using a centred difference.

Reading this equation. Symbols:

  • $G_{j+1/2}$ — conductive heat flux through the face between cells $j$ and $j+1$. Sign: $G > 0$ means downward.
  • $\lambda_{j+1/2}$ — thermal conductivity at the face. Computed from cell-centre values $\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_{j+1}$ via the harmonic mean (next).
  • $T_j - T_{j+1}$ — temperature drop from cell $j$ to cell $j+1$. If $T_j > T_{j+1}$, heat flows downward.
  • $z_{j+1} - z_j$ — centre-to-centre distance.

In plain words: heat flux at this face = (effective conductivity at the face) × (temperature drop) / (distance). Just Fourier's law $q = \lambda \Delta T/\Delta z$ written for two adjacent cells. The minus sign in Fourier's law has been absorbed into writing $T_j - T_{j+1}$ instead of $T_{j+1} - T_j$, so $G > 0$ means downward.

Why the harmonic mean for $\lambda$ at faces?

Solve the steady-state conduction problem across an interface between two materials in series. Flux must be the same on both sides:

$$q = \lambda_1 \frac{\Delta T_1}{\Delta z_1} = \lambda_2 \frac{\Delta T_2}{\Delta z_2}.$$

Reading this equation. Two materials in series — material 1 (upper), material 2 (lower). In steady state, the same flux $q$ flows through both, with each obeying Fourier's law.

Total drop is $\Delta T = \Delta T_1 + \Delta T_2$. From the relations: $\Delta T_1 = q\Delta z_1/\lambda_1$, $\Delta T_2 = q\Delta z_2/\lambda_2$. Adding:

$$\Delta T = q(\frac{\Delta z_1}{\lambda_1} + \frac{\Delta z_2}{\lambda_2}) = q \frac{\Delta z}{\lambda_{\text{eff}}},$$

where

$$\lambda_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\Delta z_1 + \Delta z_2}{\Delta z_1/\lambda_1 + \Delta z_2/\lambda_2}.$$

For two cells of equal thickness this collapses to:

$$\lambda_{j+1/2} = \frac{2 \lambda_j \lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j + \lambda_{j+1}}.$$

Status: [Derived] above. Standard convention in finite-volume codes (Patankar 1980).

Worked example. $\lambda_1 = 1.5$ (concrete deck), $\lambda_2 = 0.04$ (insulation). - Arithmetic mean: $(1.5 + 0.04)/2 = 0.77$. Wrong. - Harmonic mean: $2 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 0.04 / (1.5 + 0.04) = 0.12/1.54 = 0.078$. Correct (close to insulation).

The arithmetic mean over-estimates flux across this interface by a factor of about 10.

The semi-discrete tendency

$$C_j \Delta z_j \frac{dT_j}{dt} = G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}.$$

Status: [Derived] — discretizing the heat equation by integrating over cell $j$.

Reading this equation. Symbols: $C_j$ is heat capacity in cell $j$; $\Delta z_j$ is cell thickness; $C_j \Delta z_j$ is heat capacity per unit horizontal area; $dT_j/dt$ is rate of change of cell temperature with time (regular $d$, not $\partial$, because once space is discretised, temperature is a function of time only); $G_{j-1/2}$ is flux entering at the top; $G_{j+1/2}$ is flux leaving at the bottom.

Plain words: rate of energy accumulation in this cell = flux in at top - flux out at bottom. Semi-discrete: space discretised, time still continuous.

Wrapping up Section 6.2. Space is now discrete. We have one number per cell ($T_j$), one flux per face ($G_{j+1/2}$), and a textbook recipe (harmonic mean) for handling material jumps. The semi-discrete tendency $C_j \Delta z_j \, dT_j/dt = G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}$ is the closest a continuous-time ODE comes to the project's solver. Section 6.3 takes the final step: discretise time as well, choosing how to treat the right-hand-side at the new time level versus the old.

6.3 Section 2.3 — Building the $\theta$-method update equation

What we are doing in this subsection. Space is discrete (Section 6.2). Now we discretise time. The recipe is to integrate the semi-discrete tendency from $t^n$ to $t^{n+1}$ and approximate the integral with a quadrature rule. Three quadrature rules give three schemes — left rectangle (FTCS), right rectangle (BTCS), trapezoid (CN). All three live inside the single $\theta$-method formula. By the end of the subsection you will see exactly where the tridiagonal linear system comes from.

The semi-discrete equation is $C_j \Delta z_j dT_j/dt = R_j(t)$, where $R_j = G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}$. Integrate from $t^n$ to $t^{n+1}$:

$$C_j \Delta z_j (T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n) = \int_{t^n}^{t^{n+1}} R_j(t) dt.$$

Reading this equation. Left side: heat capacity times cell thickness times the temperature change over the step — that is the change in stored energy. Right side: integral of the rate $R_j$ over the step.

Three quadrature rules give three schemes: - Left rectangle: integral $\approx \Delta t R_j^n$. Gives FTCS. - Right rectangle: integral $\approx \Delta t R_j^{n+1}$. Gives BTCS. - Trapezoid: integral $\approx (\Delta t/2)[R_j^n + R_j^{n+1}]$. Gives CN.

In one line — the $\theta$-method update:

$$C_j \Delta z_j \frac{T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n}{\Delta t} = \alpha [G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}]^{n+1} + (1-\alpha) [G_{j-1/2} - G_{j+1/2}]^n.$$

Status: [Definition] — the project's discretisation.

Reading this equation. Left side: $C_j\Delta z_j$ multiplies the discrete time-derivative approximation $(T_j^{n+1} - T_j^n)/\Delta t$. Right side: $\alpha$-weighted average of flux divergence at time level $n+1$ (new) and $n$ (old). Notation $[\ldots]^{n+1}$ means "evaluate the bracketed expression at time level $n+1$".

Why FTCS, BTCS first-order; CN second-order

Plug into Taylor expansion around midpoint $t^n + \Delta t/2$. Let $R_m = R(t^n + \Delta t/2)$:

$$R(t^n) = R_m - \frac{\Delta t}{2} R'_m + \frac{\Delta t^2}{8} R''_m - \cdots$$

$$R(t^{n+1}) = R_m + \frac{\Delta t}{2} R'_m + \frac{\Delta t^2}{8} R''_m + \cdots$$

Average:

$$\frac{1}{2}[R(t^n) + R(t^{n+1})] = R_m + \frac{\Delta t^2}{8} R''_m + O(\Delta t^4).$$

Status: [Derived] — Taylor's theorem.

Reading this. $R_m$ is rate at midpoint. $R'_m$ is its time derivative there. The two Taylor expansions look the same except $\Delta t/2 \cdot R'_m$ has opposite signs. Averaging cancels them. Trapezoid is second-order. Single rectangles are first-order.

Linear systems and tridiagonal solves

When $\alpha > 0$, the RHS involves $T$ at level $n+1$. Each cell $j$ couples only to $j \pm 1$. Matrix is tridiagonal.

Tridiagonal systems solve in $O(N)$ via the Thomas algorithm — scipy.linalg.solve_banded((1,1), A, b).

Status: [Established algorithm] (Thomas 1949).

Wrapping up Section 6.3. The $\theta$-method is now fully assembled. For $\alpha = 0$ it is a simple arithmetic update; for $\alpha > 0$ it produces a tridiagonal linear system solved in $O(N)$ by the Thomas algorithm. Next we close the system with boundary conditions — without them the PDE has infinitely many solutions.

6.4 Section 2.4 — The boundary conditions

What we are doing in this subsection. The heat equation is second-order in $z$, so we need two BCs: one at the top of the column and one at the bottom. The choices for this project are the surface energy balance at the top (a Dirichlet-like closure where $T_s^0$ is the unknown) and a zero-flux Neumann at the bottom (heat does not leak out at $z = 2$ m). The two together close the linear system and make the solution unique.

Two main types: - Dirichlet — prescribes the value of $T$ at the boundary. - Neumann — prescribes the value of the gradient $\partial T/\partial z$ at the boundary.

Status: [Established] — standard PDE classification.

Lower boundary: zero-flux Neumann. At $z = z_{\text{top}} = 2$ m we impose $\partial T/\partial z = 0$. Discretely: $T_{N-1} = T_{N-2}$. Reading this: deepest cell ($j = N-1$) has the same temperature as the cell above ($j = N-2$), forcing the discrete gradient between them to zero, hence zero flux.

Upper boundary: Dirichlet on $T_s^0$. At cell 0 we set $T_0 = T_s^0$. The "0" superscript in $T_s^0$ is a label (depth zero), not an exponent.

Wrapping up Section 6.4. System closed: two BCs and the $\theta$-method update give a complete linear-algebra problem we can hand to SciPy at every time step. But "the simulation can be advanced step by step" does not yet mean "the simulation will remain finite as we advance it" — that question is the subject of stability analysis in Section 6.5.

6.5 Section 2.5 — Von Neumann stability analysis from scratch

What we are doing in this subsection. We answer the question "for which time steps does the scheme remain bounded?" The technique is von Neumann's: assume errors look like sinusoidal Fourier modes, plug each mode into the update equation, and compute the per-step multiplier $A$. If $|A| \le 1$ for every resolvable wavenumber, the scheme is stable; otherwise some mode grows exponentially and the simulation blows up. The result will be exactly the inequality $\nu \le 1/2$ for FTCS and "unconditional stability" for BTCS and CN — the punchline of the entire project's methodology.

Setup: linearise, idealise, then test

Von Neumann analysis works only for linear schemes on uniform grids with constant coefficients. Idealise: assume $\lambda$ and $C$ are constant, $\Delta z$ uniform, periodic boundaries.

Step 1 — Substitute a Fourier mode

Any small perturbation can be written as a sum of complex-exponential Fourier modes:

$$T_j^n = A^n e^{ikj\Delta z}.$$

Status: [Established technique] — von Neumann (1947).

Reading this expression. Decoded:

  • $T_j^n$ — temperature at cell $j$, time level $n$.
  • $A^n$ — symbol $A$ raised to $n$-th power. $A$ is the amplification factor (one complex number per wavenumber); $n$ is the time-level index. Per step, $A^n$ becomes $A^{n+1} = A \cdot A^n$ — so $A$ is the factor by which the mode is multiplied each step.
  • $e^{ikj\Delta z}$ — complex exponential. $i$ is the imaginary unit ($i^2 = -1$); $k$ is wavenumber (rad/m); $j$ is cell index; $\Delta z$ is grid spacing. By Euler's identity $e^{ikj\Delta z} = \cos(kj\Delta z) + i\sin(kj\Delta z)$ — sinusoidal pattern in space.

So $T_j^n = A^n e^{ikj\Delta z}$ is a sinusoid in space whose amplitude evolves multiplicatively in time by $A$ per step. We compute $A$ for each scheme to determine if mode grows ($|A| > 1$) or decays ($|A| < 1$).

The grid resolves $k$ from 0 to $\pi/\Delta z$, so we need $|A(\nu, k\Delta z)| \le 1$ for $k\Delta z \in [0, \pi]$.

Step 2 — Plug into FTCS

$$T_j^{n+1} = T_j^n + \frac{\kappa \Delta t}{\Delta z^2}[T_{j+1}^n - 2T_j^n + T_{j-1}^n].$$

Reading this equation. Each cell's new value = old value + correction. The correction is $(\kappa\Delta t/\Delta z^2)$ — the diffusion number $\nu$ — times the centred-difference second derivative (in the bracket).

Define $\nu = \kappa \Delta t/\Delta z^2$. Substitute the Fourier mode:

$$A^{n+1} e^{ikj\Delta z} = A^n e^{ikj\Delta z} + \nu A^n[e^{ik(j+1)\Delta z} - 2 e^{ikj\Delta z} + e^{ik(j-1)\Delta z}].$$

Reading this step. Each $T_j^n$ becomes $A^n e^{ikj\Delta z}$; each $T_{j\pm 1}^n$ becomes $A^n e^{ik(j\pm 1)\Delta z}$.

Divide both sides by $A^n e^{ikj\Delta z}$:

$$A = 1 + \nu[e^{ik\Delta z} - 2 + e^{-ik\Delta z}].$$

Use $e^{ix} + e^{-ix} = 2\cos x$:

$$A_{\text{FTCS}}(\nu, k\Delta z) = 1 - 2\nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z).$$

Status: [Derived] above. Established result Notes #9 (Pure Diffusion).

Reading this final formula. $A_{\text{FTCS}}$ depends on $\nu$ and $k\Delta z$. The factor $(1 - \cos k\Delta z)$ ranges from 0 (at $k\Delta z = 0$) to 2 (at $k\Delta z = \pi$). At long wavelengths $A \approx 1$. At the shortest wavelength, $A = 1 - 4\nu$ — negative or large in magnitude depending on $\nu$.

Step 3 — Plug into BTCS

$$T_j^{n+1} = T_j^n + \nu[T_{j+1}^{n+1} - 2T_j^{n+1} + T_{j-1}^{n+1}].$$

Reading this equation. Same form as FTCS, but correction uses $T$ at level $n+1$ — implicit.

Substitute the Fourier mode:

$$A^{n+1} = A^n + \nu A^{n+1}[2\cos k\Delta z - 2].$$

Divide by $A^n$:

$$A = 1 + A\nu[2\cos k\Delta z - 2] = 1 - 2A\nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z).$$

Solve for $A$:

$$A[1 + 2\nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z)] = 1.$$

$$A_{\text{BTCS}}(\nu, k\Delta z) = \frac{1}{1 + 2\nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z)}.$$

Status: [Derived] above.

Reading this final formula. $A_{\text{BTCS}}$ is one over a positive number $\ge 1$. Always between 0 and 1 — strictly damping, regardless of $\nu$. That is BTCS's unconditional stability.

Step 4 — Plug into CN

$$T_j^{n+1} = T_j^n + \frac{\nu}{2}[T_{j+1}^n - 2T_j^n + T_{j-1}^n] + \frac{\nu}{2}[T_{j+1}^{n+1} - 2T_j^{n+1} + T_{j-1}^{n+1}].$$

Reading this equation. Two corrections, each scaled by $\nu/2$ — one at old time level, one at new.

Let $h = \nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z)$. Substitute. The two bracketed groups become $-2A^n h$ and $-2A^{n+1} h$:

$$A^{n+1} = A^n - A^n h - A^{n+1} h.$$

Divide by $A^n$:

$$A = 1 - h - hA.$$

$$A(1 + h) = 1 - h.$$

$$A_{\text{CN}}(\nu, k\Delta z) = \frac{1 - h}{1 + h} = \frac{1 - \nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z)}{1 + \nu(1 - \cos k\Delta z)}.$$

Status: [Derived] above. Established result Notes #9 (Implicit Diffusion).

Reading this final formula. Form $(1-h)/(1+h)$ where $h \ge 0$. For $h \in [0, 1]$, $A \in [0, 1]$. For $h > 1$, $A < 0$ but $|A| < 1$. As $h \to \infty$, $A \to -1$. So at large $\nu$, the worst wave is preserved in magnitude but flips sign — the over-damping pathology.

What $|A| \le 1$ means

After $n$ steps, amplitude is $A^n$. If $|A| > 1$, the mode grows exponentially. If $|A| \le 1$, the mode holds steady or decays.

Why $k\Delta z = \pi$ is the worst case

The factor $(1 - \cos k\Delta z)$ ranges from 0 (at $k\Delta z = 0$) to 2 (at $k\Delta z = \pi$). Worst case at $k\Delta z = \pi$, the 2-$\Delta z$ wave. Substituting:

$$A_{\text{FTCS}}(\nu, \pi) = 1 - 4\nu, \quad A_{\text{BTCS}}(\nu, \pi) = \frac{1}{1 + 4\nu}, \quad A_{\text{CN}}(\nu, \pi) = \frac{1 - 2\nu}{1 + 2\nu}.$$

Status: [Derived] by setting $k\Delta z = \pi$.

Reading the stability conclusions

FTCS at the worst wave: $A = 1 - 4\nu$. For $|A| \le 1$ need $\nu \le 1/2$. Conditionally stable with bound

$$\nu \le \frac{1}{2}, \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad \Delta t \le \frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta z^2}{\kappa}.$$

BTCS: $|A| < 1$ always. Unconditionally stable, strictly damping.

CN: $|A| \le 1$ always. Unconditionally stable. As $\nu \to \infty$, $A \to -1$ — over-damping pathology.

7.3 Section 3.3 — The surface energy balance and Newton iteration

What we are doing in this subsection. The substrate solver advances $T_j$ at every cell from $n$ to $n+1$ given $T_s^0$. But who decides $T_s^0$? Answer: the surface energy balance. It is a single nonlinear algebraic equation in one unknown ($T_s^0$) that we solve by Newton iteration at every time step. This subsection makes that equation concrete and shows how Newton iteration converges in a handful of evaluations.

The SEB

$$R_n(T_s^0) - H(T_s^0) - LE(T_s^0) - G(T_s^0) = 0.$$

Status: [Established].

Reading this equation. Notation $R_n(T_s^0)$ shows that $R_n$ depends on $T_s^0$. Same for $H$, $LE$, $G$. The SEB is one equation in one unknown — $T_s^0$.

Net radiation:

$$R_n = (1 - \alpha_s) S_\downarrow + \varepsilon_s L_\downarrow - \varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^4.$$

Status: [Established].

Reading this equation. Three terms:

  • $(1 - \alpha_s) S_\downarrow$ — absorbed shortwave. $\alpha_s$ (Greek "alpha" with subscript "s") is the surface shortwave albedo (dimensionless, 0 to 1) — fraction of sunlight reflected. $1 - \alpha_s$ is fraction absorbed. $S_\downarrow$ (read "S down arrow") is incoming downward shortwave (W/m²).
  • $\varepsilon_s L_\downarrow$ — absorbed longwave. $\varepsilon_s$ (Greek "epsilon" with subscript "s") is the surface longwave emissivity. By Kirchhoff's law, emissivity = absorptivity. $L_\downarrow$ is incoming downward longwave from atmosphere.
  • $-\varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^4$ — outgoing longwave emitted by the surface, given by the Stefan–Boltzmann law: a body at temperature $T$ radiates at rate $\sigma T^4$ per unit area. $\sigma = 5.67\times 10^{-8}$ W/m²/K⁴ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. Minus sign because energy leaves the surface.

In $(T_s^0)^4$, the 4 IS an exponent, but the 0 in $T_s^0$ is a label (depth). Always check context.

The Stefan–Boltzmann $T^4$ is what makes the SEB nonlinear.

Sensible heat flux:

$$H = \rho c_p \frac{T_s^0 - T_a}{r_a}, \qquad r_a = \frac{1}{C_H U}.$$

Status of $H$: [Established] — bulk-aerodynamic formula. Status of $r_a$: [Definition].

Reading these equations. Symbols:

  • $\rho$ (Greek "rho") $\approx 1.2$ kg/m³ — air density.
  • $c_p = 1005$ J/kg/K — specific heat of air at constant pressure.
  • $\rho c_p$ = volumetric heat capacity of air, about 1206 J/m³/K. Compare to substrate's ~$2\times 10^6$ — air is a much poorer thermal reservoir.
  • $T_s^0 - T_a$ — surface-air temperature difference. Drives heat exchange.
  • $r_a$ — aerodynamic resistance (s/m). Bigger $r_a$ → weaker coupling.
  • $C_H = 5\times 10^{-3}$ — bulk transfer coefficient (dimensionless, turbulence-derived).
  • $U = 3$ m/s — wind speed.

In plain words: $H$ is proportional to surface-air temperature difference, divided by aerodynamic resistance. The resistance is inversely proportional to wind speed — windy days couple surface and air more strongly. Standard "bulk aerodynamic" parameterization.

Latent heat flux:

$$LE = 0 \quad (\text{strict-impervious assumption}).$$

Status: [Project-specific assumption].

Ground heat flux:

$$G = \lambda_{1/2} \frac{T_s^0 - T_1}{z_1 - z_0}.$$

Status: [Definition].

Reading this equation. Same as discrete-flux formula from Section 6.2, applied at the top face (between surface at $z_0 = 0$ and first interior cell at $z_1$). $\lambda_{1/2}$ is the harmonic mean conductivity at that face.

Why the SEB is nonlinear

The $(T_s^0)^4$ term makes the SEB nonlinear in $T_s^0$. We must find the root of $F(T_s^0) = R_n - H - LE - G$ by Newton iteration.

Newton's method

Start with a guess $T_{s,\text{old}}^0$. Approximate $F$ linearly:

$$F(T) \approx F(T_{s,\text{old}}^0) + F'(T_{s,\text{old}}^0)(T - T_{s,\text{old}}^0).$$

Status: [Derived] — first-order Taylor expansion.

Reading this equation. Tangent-line approximation: $F(T)$ near our guess is approximately the value at the guess plus the slope times how far away $T$ is.

Set this approximation to zero and solve:

$$T_{s,\text{new}}^0 = T_{s,\text{old}}^0 - \frac{F(T_{s,\text{old}}^0)}{F'(T_{s,\text{old}}^0)}.$$

Status: [Established] — Newton's method (Newton 1669, Raphson 1690).

Reading this equation. Geometrically: find where the tangent line crosses zero; use that as the new guess. Iterate until $|T_{s,\text{new}}^0 - T_{s,\text{old}}^0| < 10^{-4}$ K. Quadratic convergence.

The full analytical Jacobian

$$\frac{dF}{dT_s} = \frac{dR_n}{dT_s} - \frac{dH}{dT_s} - \frac{dG}{dT_s}.$$

Term by term:

$$\frac{dR_n}{dT_s} = -4 \varepsilon_s \sigma (T_s^0)^3.$$

Status: [Derived] — power rule on $T^4$.

$$\frac{dH}{dT_s} = \frac{\rho c_p}{r_a}.$$

Status: [Derived] — $H$ is linear in $T_s$.

$$\frac{dG}{dT_s} = \frac{\lambda_{1/2}}{z_1 - z_0}.$$

Status: [Derived] — $G$ is linear in $T_s$.

Wrapping up Section 7.3. Every step we solve one SEB nonlinear equation by Newton iteration to find $T_s^0$, then advance the column. The Jacobian is analytic — no finite-difference derivative needed — so Newton converges quadratically in 3–4 iterations. The order-of-operations (column then SEB) is the source of the operator-splitting error explored in Part 9.

8.1 Section 4.1 — The damping-depth verification

What we are doing in this subsection. Before trusting the solver on the harder Test 2, we verify it on a problem with a known analytical solution: a uniform semi-infinite substrate with sinusoidal Dirichlet surface forcing. The textbook answer is the exponentially-decaying-with-depth, phase-shifted-with-depth wave. If the numerical solver reproduces that answer with the right amplitude decay and the right $\pi/4$ phase lead between $G$ and $T_s$, we have evidence the solver is mathematically correct.

The analytical solution and the $\pi/4$ phase lead — derived

For a semi-infinite uniform substrate with sinusoidal Dirichlet forcing $T_s(0, t) = \bar{T} + A\cos\omega t$, we solve

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \kappa \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial z^2}, \qquad T(0, t) = \bar{T} + A\cos\omega t, \qquad T \to \bar{T} \text{ as } z \to \infty.$$

Status: [Established theoretical problem].

Reading this setup. Three things: (1) the diffusion equation; (2) surface boundary condition — temperature at depth zero is a sinusoid in time; (3) deep boundary condition — temperature returns to mean as we go deep.

Ansatz. Try the complex form

$$T(z, t) = \bar{T} + \mathrm{Re}[A e^{i(\omega t - Kz)}],$$

where $K$ is a complex wavenumber.

Reading this ansatz. Notation $\mathrm{Re}[\cdot]$ means "real part of". The complex exponential $e^{i(\omega t - Kz)}$ encodes a wave that propagates in $z$ and oscillates in $t$. Letting $K$ be complex allows the wave to also decay with depth.

Substituting into the PDE:

$$i\omega A e^{i(\omega t - Kz)} = \kappa(-K^2) A e^{i(\omega t - Kz)}.$$

Reading this step. Differentiate ansatz with respect to $t$: factor of $i\omega$. Differentiate twice with respect to $z$: factor of $(-iK)^2 = -K^2$.

Cancel the common factor:

$$i\omega = -\kappa K^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad K^2 = -\frac{i\omega}{\kappa}.$$

Status: [Derived] — direct substitution.

Take the square root. Recall $\sqrt{-i} = (1 - i)/\sqrt{2}$:

$$K = \pm\frac{1 - i}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\omega/\kappa}.$$

Reading this step. Find $K$ such that $K^2 = -i\omega/\kappa$. Write $-i$ in polar form as $e^{-i\pi/2}$. Square root is $e^{-i\pi/4} = (1 - i)/\sqrt{2}$. Take the root that gives decay as $z \to \infty$:

$$K = \frac{1 - i}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\omega/\kappa} = \frac{1 - i}{d}, \qquad d = \sqrt{\frac{2\kappa}{\omega}}.$$

Now compute $-iKz$:

$$-iKz = -i(1-i)\frac{z}{d}.$$

Use $-i(1 - i) = -i + i^2 = -i - 1 = -(1 + i)$:

$$-iKz = -\frac{(1 + i)z}{d} = -\frac{z}{d} - i\frac{z}{d}.$$

Reading this step. Multiply: $-i \cdot 1 = -i$; $-i \cdot (-i) = i^2 = -1$. So $-i(1-i) = -i - 1$. Multiply by $z/d$.

So

$$e^{i(\omega t - Kz)} = e^{i\omega t} e^{-iKz} = e^{i\omega t} e^{-z/d} e^{-iz/d} = e^{-z/d} e^{i(\omega t - z/d)}.$$

Take real part:

$$T_s(z, t) = \bar{T} + A e^{-z/d}\cos(\omega t - z/d), \qquad d = \sqrt{2\kappa/\omega}.$$

Status: [Derived] above. Established result.

Reading this final formula. Two depth-dependent factors: exponential decay $e^{-z/d}$ shrinks amplitude with depth; cosine $\cos(\omega t - z/d)$ has depth-dependent phase shift. Deeper points feel the daily cycle later and weaker.

Surface flux

$G(0, t) = -\lambda \partial T/\partial z$ at $z = 0$. Compute the derivative:

$$\frac{\partial T}{\partial z} = A e^{-z/d}[-\frac{1}{d}\cos(\omega t - z/d) + \frac{1}{d}\sin(\omega t - z/d)].$$

Status: [Derived] — chain rule.

At $z = 0$:

$$\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0} = \frac{A}{d}[-\cos\omega t + \sin\omega t].$$

Therefore

$$G(0, t) = -\lambda\frac{A}{d}[-\cos\omega t + \sin\omega t] = \lambda\frac{A}{d}[\cos\omega t - \sin\omega t].$$

Use the identity $\cos x - \sin x = \sqrt{2}\cos(x + \pi/4)$:

$$G(0, t) = \lambda\frac{A}{d}\sqrt{2}\cos(\omega t + \pi/4).$$

Verifying the trig identity. $\sqrt{2}\cos(x + \pi/4) = \sqrt{2}[\cos x \cos(\pi/4) - \sin x \sin(\pi/4)] = \cos x - \sin x$. ✓

Reading the final formula. $G$ is sinusoidal with amplitude $\lambda A\sqrt{2}/d$, phase $\omega t + \pi/4$. The $+\pi/4$ phase lead means $G$ peaks $\pi/4$ in phase before $T_s$ peaks. For 24 hours, $\pi/4$ = 3 hours. If $T_s$ peaks at noon, $G$ peaks at 9 AM.

Wrapping up the analytical derivation. We now have the closed-form prediction: an exponentially-decaying-with-depth, phase-lagged-with-depth surface temperature wave, and a surface ground heat flux that leads the surface temperature by exactly $\pi/4$ in phase (3 hours of 24). The ordering is counter-intuitive. The most diffusive substrate (concrete deck, $\kappa = 7.14 \times 10^{-7}$) is the most restrictive — $\Delta t_{\mathrm{crit}} = 17$ s. "Stiff" in plain English. An equation is "stiff" when the time-step constraint is set by the fastest-evolving component, regardless of how small that component is in size. The top 5 mm of concrete deck has $\kappa = 7 \times 10^{-7}$ m²/s — it responds quickly to surface forcing. That single thin cell sets the bound for the whole column. Adding 200 cm of slow-responding drywall below does not help.

Why operational models cannot use FTCS at all. Mesoscale weather models use $\Delta t = 60$ s. Regional climate models use $\Delta t = 600$ s. Both exceed every substrate's FTCS bound by factors of 3 to 35. So unless you give up on resolving the 8–14 cm diurnal damping depth (which requires $\Delta z \sim 0.5$ cm), FTCS is out. This forces the entire field toward BTCS or Crank–Nicolson — and the rest of this document is about what errors those implicit schemes introduce in exchange.

9.2 Section 5.2 — The operator-splitting error

What we are doing in this subsection. Sections 8.2 and 8.3 showed that BTCS and CN both have first-order error in $\Delta t$, even though CN is supposed to be second-order. Section 9.2 explains where the first-order error comes from: it is the splitting between the column update and the SEB update. The column is solved holding $T_s^0$ fixed, then $T_s^0$ is updated holding the column fixed. This split is mathematically a leading-order commutator error — first-order in $\Delta t$ regardless of the per-substep accuracy.

How the splitting works

Each step has two sub-steps: - Sub-step A — column update. Hold $T_s^0$ fixed. Solve heat conduction equation in column. - Sub-step B — SEB update. With new interior values fixed, solve SEB by Newton iteration for new $T_s^0$.

Why this produces over-amplification

At sunset $T_s^0$ drops fast, say from 35 to 25 °C in $\Delta t = 600$ s. Sub-step A holds $T_s^0$ at 35 → column relaxes to that. Sub-step B computes new $T_s^0 = 25$ but column is still at 35. Gradient at top half-level is steeper than truth → $G$ larger.

Lecture 10: "Implicit scheme brings the local profiles into equilibrium, but without accounting for the other tendencies it can be the wrong equilibrium." Exactly the project's pathology.

Why both BTCS and CN show first-order scaling

Splitting error is first-order in $\Delta t$ regardless of per-substep scheme.

What we are doing in this subsection. Section 9.2 told us the error is first-order in $\Delta t$. But the magnitude differs across substrates: 40% on asphalt, 41% on roof, 19% on soil. Strang splitting

Option A: Fully coupled SEB-row solve. Augment tridiagonal column system with one extra row.

Option B: Strang splitting. Symmetric reorganisation:

$$\mathrm{A}(\Delta t/2) \to \mathrm{B}(\Delta t) \to \mathrm{A}(\Delta t/2).$$

Status: [Established technique] — Strang (1968).

Reading this. $\mathrm{A}$ = column update; $\mathrm{B}$ = SEB update. Half a column step, then full SEB step, then another half column step.

The standard split approximates

$$e^{(\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{B})\Delta t} \approx e^{\mathrm{B}\Delta t}e^{\mathrm{A}\Delta t},$$

with leading error proportional to $[\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}]\Delta t^2/2$ — first-order. Strang form

$$e^{(\mathrm{A}+\mathrm{B})\Delta t} \approx e^{\mathrm{A}\Delta t/2}e^{\mathrm{B}\Delta t}e^{\mathrm{A}\Delta t/2}$$

cancels the leading commutator by symmetry — second-order in splitting.

Status: [Derived] — Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula.

10.3 Section 6.2 — Gradient-boosted regression and SHAP

Gradient boosting

$$\hat{y}_m(x) = \hat{y}_{m-1}(x) + \eta \mathrm{tree}_m(x).$$

Final:

$$\hat{y}_M(x) = \hat{y}_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta \mathrm{tree}_m(x).$$

Status: [Established algorithm] — Friedman (2001).

Reading these equations. Symbols:

  • $\hat{y}_m(x)$ — model prediction after $m$ rounds at input $x$. Hat distinguishes prediction from true target.
  • $\hat{y}_0$ — initial prediction (mean of $y$).
  • $\mathrm{tree}_m(x)$ — $m$-th decision tree's prediction at $x$.
  • $\eta$ (Greek "eta") — learning rate, small positive number (project: 0.05).
  • $M$ — total number of trees (project: 200).
  • $\sum_{m=1}^{M}$ — sum from $m = 1$ to $m = M$.

In plain words: start with mean, iteratively add small corrections (decision trees) tuned to fix errors of cumulative model so far.

SHAP

For each prediction and each feature $f$:

$$\hat{y}(x) = \text{baseline} + \sum_f \phi_f(x).$$

Status: [Established] — Shapley (1953); Lundberg & Lee (2017).

Reading this equation. $\hat{y}(x)$ is prediction for input $x$. Baseline = mean of $y$. $\phi_f(x)$ is contribution of feature $f$ to this prediction. Sum over features + baseline gives prediction.

$\phi$ (Greek "phi") is conventional in cooperative game theory for the Shapley value. Positive $\phi_f$ → feature pushed prediction up. Mean absolute $\overline{|\phi_f|}$ over all data points = global feature importance.

Model fit metrics

  • In-sample $R^2 = 0.96$.
  • 5-fold cross-validated $R^2 = 0.61$.

Part 14. Common confusions

14.1 Why is $\alpha$ used for two different things?

$\theta$-method weight vs surface albedo $\alpha_s$. Subscript disambiguates.

14.2 Why $\kappa$ instead of $\lambda$ in the diffusivity form?

Conductivity form always valid; diffusivity form requires constant $\lambda, C$.

14.3 Why does $\nu$ matter and not $\Delta t$ alone?

Stability is dimensionless. $\nu$ is the right combination.

14.4 Why does $k\Delta z$ go up to $\pi$?

Finest wavelength is $2\Delta z$, so $k_{\max}\Delta z = \pi$.

14.5 Why is FTCS first-order but centred-space second-order?

FTCS = Forward in Time (first-order), Centred in Space (second-order). Overall first-order.

14.6 Why did the abstract say FTCS blows up on all substrates?

Corrected — bare soil at $\Delta t = 600$ s completes with substantial error.

14.7 Why is bare soil hottest if asphalt has lowest albedo?

Low $\kappa$ + $LE = 0$ concentrates heat at surface.

14.8 Why is operator-splitting error first-order even though CN is second-order?

CN's second-order applies per-substep. Splitting error is separate, first-order.

14.9 Why is $\kappa_{\text{top}}$ dominant SHAP feature when admittance governs storage?

$\kappa_{\text{top}}$ governs response rate; splitting error is about rate, not capacity.

14.10 What would I gain by using a fully-coupled solver?

Eliminate first-order splitting error. 1.1 K BTCS error on asphalt could drop to 0.1–0.2 K.
